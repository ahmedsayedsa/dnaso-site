The 7,500-watt generator is the most popular size in America for a reason: it's the sweet spot where a portable unit can genuinely run a house — fridge, freezer, well pump, lights, internet, and then some — without stepping up to a permanently installed standby that costs five figures. It's also the size where people get into trouble, because "7,500 watts" doesn't mean what most buyers think it means.

This is the full picture: what a 7,500W generator can actually run, the running-versus-starting-watts trap, three worked scenarios with real math, what it can't handle, and which models are worth your money.

## First: 7,500 running watts, not 9,500

Every 7,500W portable is also sold with a bigger number on the box — usually 9,375 or 9,500 "starting" or "peak" watts. Here's the distinction that matters:

- **Running watts (7,500):** what the generator can produce continuously, all day. This is your budget.
- **Starting watts (9,375–9,500):** the short surge it can deliver for a few seconds when a motor starts. This is your emergency fund.

You size your loads against the 7,500 running watts. The surge capacity exists to absorb motor startups — fridge compressors, well pumps, furnace blowers — without tripping. If your steady load is 7,800 watts, you're over budget even though you're "under" the peak number, and the generator will run hot, burn oil, and eventually trip or fail.

The golden rule: **plan for no more than 80% of running watts as your continuous load** — about 6,000 watts on a 7,500W unit. That leaves headroom for motor surges and keeps the engine in its happy zone. For the full sizing method, see [What Size Generator Do I Need to Run My House?](/articles/what-size-generator-run-house/).

## Room-by-room: what things actually draw

Two numbers per appliance: running watts (steady draw) and starting watts (the brief surge when a motor kicks on). Resistive loads like heaters and incandescent lights have no surge — what you see is what you get. Anything with a motor surges 2–3x on startup.

|---|---|---|
| Refrigerator (modern, 18–25 cu ft) | 150–250 | 800–1,200 |
| Chest freezer | 100–200 | 600–1,000 |
| Microwave | 1,000–1,200 | 1,000–1,200 (no surge) |
| Coffee maker | 800–1,200 | none |
| Toaster oven | 1,200–1,500 | none |
| Electric stove (one burner) | 1,200–2,500 | none |
| Dishwasher (heating cycle) | 1,200–1,800 | 1,500–2,000 |

Study this table before buying anything. The items that surprise people: the electric water heater (4,500W — more than half the generator by itself), the electric dryer (similar), and the well pump's starting surge (a 1 HP pump can momentarily demand 6,000W — nearly the whole generator — just to start).

## Scenario 1: Essentials only (~3,500W)

The "keep the food cold and the basement dry" setup. This is what most people actually need in a 1–3 day outage.

| Load | Running watts |
|---|---|
| Refrigerator | 200 |
| Chest freezer | 150 |
| Sump pump (cycling) | 800 (intermittent) |
| 10 LED lights | 100 |
| Wi-Fi + modem | 30 |
| Phone/laptop charging | 100 |
| Gas furnace blower (winter) | 800 |
| **Total steady load** | **~2,200–3,000** |

Worst-case surge moment: fridge compressor (1,200W surge) + sump pump kicking on (2,000W surge) while everything else runs. Total momentary: roughly 5,500–6,000W — comfortably inside the 9,500W surge capacity, and the steady load sits at about 40% of the 7,500W rating. The generator sips fuel at this load — roughly 0.5–0.65 gal/hr — and runs cool and quiet-ish all day.

This scenario leaves enormous headroom. You could add a microwave (1,200W) for meals or a window AC (1,200W) in summer and still be under 5,000W steady.

## Scenario 2: Comfort mode (~6,000W)

The "live almost normally, just manage the big stuff" setup. This is the 80%-of-capacity target and where a 7,500W unit earns its keep.

| Load | Running watts |
|---|---|
| Refrigerator | 200 |
| Chest freezer | 150 |
| Well pump (1/2 HP, cycling) | 1,000 (intermittent) |
| Gas furnace blower | 800 |
| 2 window ACs (10,000 BTU each) — summer alternative | 2,400 |
| Microwave (intermittent) | 1,200 |
| 15 LED lights | 150 |
| Wi-Fi, TVs, chargers, laptops | 400 |
| Washing machine (intermittent) | 700 |
| **Total steady load** | **~5,500–6,200** |

The management rule here: don't run the microwave and the washing machine's agitation cycle while both window AC compressors are starting. Stagger the big intermittent loads and you stay inside budget. The well pump's 3,000W starting surge is the biggest single event — if it coincides with both AC compressors starting (unlikely but possible), you could briefly touch 8,000–9,000W total, which the surge capacity absorbs for the second or two it lasts.

Fuel burn at this load: roughly 0.8–1.1 gal/hr. On a 6.5-gallon tank, that's a refuel every 6–8 hours. For the full fuel math, see [How Much Fuel Does a Generator Use? Real Consumption Numbers](/articles/generator-fuel-consumption/).

## Scenario 3: Almost everything (~7,200W) — the edge

This is the maximum sensible load: everything in Scenario 2 plus an electric water heater recovery cycle, or the electric dryer, or a third window AC. Pick ONE big resistive load at a time.

| Load | Running watts |
|---|---|
| Everything in Scenario 2 | ~5,800 |
| Electric water heater (recovery) | 4,500 |
| **Combined** | **10,300 — OVER BUDGET** |

That's the point: you can't have it all at once. The technique is rotation — run the water heater for its 45-minute recovery cycle with the ACs and dryer off, then switch back. Water stays hot for hours in an insulated tank; you don't need the heater and the dryer simultaneously.

A workable rotation schedule:

1. **Morning:** water heater on (4,500W) + fridge/freezer + lights — ~5,200W. Showers done by 8.
2. **Midday:** water heater off, window ACs on, laundry running — ~6,000W.
3. **Evening:** cooking (microwave instead of stove), ACs on, everything else normal — ~5,500W.
4. **Night:** ACs on low or off, fridge + furnace + lights — ~3,000W. Generator sips fuel overnight.

This is how people live comfortably for a week on a 7,500W portable. It's not automatic — it takes a day to learn the rhythm — but it works.

## What a 7,500W generator CAN'T run

**Central AC (without a soft starter).** This is the big one, and it deserves the full explanation because it's the most common expensive mistake.

A 3-ton central AC runs at about 3,500 watts — well within a 7,500W generator's capacity. But starting the compressor demands 9,000–12,000 watts for the first second or two (locked-rotor amps, typically 60–80A at 240V). The generator's 9,500W surge rating can't deliver it. The compressor stalls, voltage collapses, and either the generator's breaker trips or — worse — the compressor sits there trying to start against low voltage and cooks its windings. Low-voltage start attempts are a leading cause of compressor death.

A **soft starter** (MicroAir EasyStart is the well-known brand, ~$300–400 plus installation) reduces the starting surge by 60–70%, bringing a 3-ton unit's startup down to roughly 3,500–4,500W — which a 7,500W generator handles. With a soft starter installed, central AC on a 7,500W portable goes from "absolutely not" to "yes, with load management." Without one, don't try it. The complete breakdown is in [What Size Generator Do I Need to Run Central AC?](/articles/what-size-generator-central-ac/).

**Also off the list:**

- **Tankless electric water heaters** (18,000–28,000W) — not even close, and no portable generator runs these.
- **Electric range + dryer + water heater simultaneously** — any two of the three big resistive loads at once exceeds capacity.
- **5-ton central AC** — even with a soft starter, the running load (~5,500W) plus everything else leaves no margin on a 7,500W unit.
- **Whole-house electric heat** — a 10kW electric furnace strip alone exceeds the generator.

## Electrical safety: read this before you plug in anything

A 7,500W generator produces enough power to kill you and enough to burn your house down if connected wrong. The non-negotiable rules:

- **Never plug a generator directly into a wall outlet** ("backfeeding"). It energizes the utility lines outside your house and can electrocute lineworkers trying to restore power. It's also illegal in most jurisdictions. The only safe ways to power house circuits are a **transfer switch** or an **interlock kit** installed on your panel — see [Transfer Switch vs Interlock Kit: Cost, Code, and Safety](/articles/transfer-switch-vs-interlock-kit/).
- **Run it outdoors, 20+ feet from the house**, exhaust pointed away. Carbon monoxide is odorless and kills in minutes. Never in a garage, basement, or under a deck — even with the door open.
- **Let the engine cool before refueling.** Shut it down, wait 15 minutes, then refuel. Gasoline on a 500°F exhaust is exactly as bad as it sounds.
- **Use heavy-gauge extension cords** (12-gauge minimum, 10-gauge for long runs) rated for outdoor use. Undersized cords overheat, drop voltage, and can melt.
- **Keep it dry.** Never operate in rain without a purpose-built generator tent or cover. Water and 240V don't negotiate.
- **Ground it** per the manufacturer's instructions — most portables driving a panel through a transfer switch use the house ground, but cord-and-plug use may require a grounding rod depending on the model and local code.

If any of this is new to you, have an electrician install the transfer switch or interlock kit and walk you through the startup sequence once. A one-hour service call is the cheapest insurance in this entire project.
