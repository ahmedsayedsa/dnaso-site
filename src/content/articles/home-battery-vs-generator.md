**Quick answer:** For short outages and silent, maintenance-free backup, a home battery wins — one 13.5 kWh unit ($9,000–$11,000 installed, roughly 30% less after the federal tax credit) runs essentials for 13–20 hours. For multi-day outages or whole-home loads like central AC, a generator wins on runtime and cost per watt — a 22 kW standby is $10,000–$15,000 installed and runs indefinitely on natural gas. Solar owners tilt toward the battery; everyone else should start with how long their outages actually last.

For decades, "backup power" meant a generator, full stop. Batteries were too expensive and too weak to be a real option. That's changed. A modern home battery can carry a house through an outage silently, with zero fuel and zero maintenance — but the math only works in certain situations. Here's the honest comparison, with real 2026 numbers.

## How Home Batteries Actually Work

A home battery is a big lithium battery bank wired into your electrical panel through an inverter and, usually, a gateway that disconnects you from the grid during an outage (islanding). When the power goes out, it switches over in milliseconds — faster than a UPS, so your computers and clocks don't even notice.

Two numbers matter, and people mix them up constantly:

- **Capacity (kWh):** how much energy the battery holds. A Tesla Powerwall 3 holds 13.5 kWh. Think of this as the size of the gas tank.
- **Power output (kW):** how fast it can deliver that energy. The Powerwall 3 delivers 11.5 kW continuous. Think of this as the engine size.

You need both to be big enough. A battery with huge capacity but weak output can't start your AC compressor. A battery with huge output but small capacity dies in an hour. For whole-home backup, both numbers have to clear your home's peak demand and your total energy need over the outage.

## The Cost Reality

Let's put real numbers on the table.

**Home batteries (installed, 2026):**

- **Tesla Powerwall 3:** roughly $9,000–$11,000 installed per unit (13.5 kWh, 11.5 kW). The unit alone is around $7,500–$8,000; installation, gateway, and permits make up the rest.
- **EcoFlow Delta Pro Ultra:** about $5,800–$6,500 for the base unit (6 kWh, 7.2 kW output). Modular — stack batteries to reach up to 30 kWh. Full whole-home setups with smart panel typically land $8,000–$12,000 installed.
- **Anker Solix X1:** roughly $7,000–$9,000 per 5 kWh unit installed; scales modularly.

Here's the part most marketing glosses over: **one battery rarely covers a whole house.** A single 13.5 kWh Powerwall gives you a few hours of normal whole-home use — maybe 3–5 hours for an average home pulling 3–4 kW continuously, less with the AC running. For true whole-home backup or multi-day coverage, most homes need two units ($18,000–$22,000 installed) or a stacked modular system.

If you only need essentials — fridge, lights, phones, router, maybe a gas furnace blower — one unit stretches much further, often 10–18 hours.

**Generators (for comparison):**

- 7,500–9,500W portable: $700–$1,500 unit, $500–$1,500 more for an interlock/transfer switch installed. All-in around $1,500–$3,000.
- 22–26 kW standby: $10,000–$15,000 installed.
- 4,500W inverter: $700–$1,200.

So on upfront cost, a single battery lands in the same neighborhood as an installed standby generator — but covers fewer loads and fewer hours. Two batteries cost roughly double a standby setup. That's the gap you have to justify.

## The 30% Tax Credit: The Number That Changes the Math

Here's what makes batteries competitive: the federal **Investment Tax Credit (ITC)** for energy storage. Standalone battery storage (3 kWh or larger) qualifies for a **30% federal tax credit** under current law — no solar panels required.

What qualifies:

- The battery itself, the inverter, wiring, gateway/islanding hardware, and installation labor directly tied to the storage system.
- It must be a new installation (not used equipment), at least 3 kWh capacity — virtually every home battery qualifies.

**Math example:** You install two batteries and related hardware for $15,000 total. 30% ITC = **$4,500 credit** against your federal tax liability. Your net cost drops to $10,500.

A few honest caveats:

- It's a credit against tax you owe, not a check. If your federal tax liability is only $2,000, you get $2,000 this year (the remainder can generally carry forward — confirm with a tax professional).
- Some states add their own incentives (California's SGIP, for example, has paid thousands per installation historically, though funding fluctuates). Check your state's current programs — they change.
- Generators get **no** comparable federal credit. Fuel, maintenance, and the unit itself are all after-tax dollars.

After the credit, a $15,000 battery system costs about what a mid-range standby generator costs installed. That's when the comparison gets interesting.

## Battery Degradation and Warranties: The Fine Print

Lithium batteries wear out — slowly, but measurably. Every home battery warranty is built around this, and you should read the terms before buying:

- **Tesla Powerwall 3:** 10-year warranty, guaranteeing at least 70% capacity retention. Unlimited cycles for solar self-consumption and backup use.
- **EcoFlow Delta Pro Ultra:** 5-year warranty (extendable), rated for ~3,500 cycles to 80% capacity.
- **Anker Solix X1:** 10-year warranty, ~6,000 cycles rated.

What "70% at 10 years" means in practice: your 13.5 kWh battery is warrantied to still hold at least ~9.5 kWh after a decade. Most owners see better than the warranty floor. But this is a real cost of ownership that generators don't have in the same form — a generator's engine can be rebuilt; a degraded battery gets replaced at near-full price.

Cycle life matters more if you're using the battery daily (see time-of-use arbitrage below). For backup-only use — a few dozen partial cycles a year — degradation is minimal and the battery will likely outlive its warranty with capacity to spare.

## The Daily Payback: Time-of-Use Arbitrage

Here's the argument that closes the cost gap for some homeowners. If your utility charges time-of-use rates — say $0.35/kWh during 4–9pm peak and $0.12/kWh overnight — a battery can earn money every single day:

- Charge overnight (or from solar midday) at the cheap rate.
- Discharge during the expensive evening peak to run your house.
- A 13.5 kWh battery cycling ~10 kWh daily through a $0.20/kWh spread saves roughly $2/day — about $700/year.

That's not going to pay off the battery alone, but combined with the 30% tax credit and outage protection, it turns the battery from a pure insurance purchase into an asset that works daily. A generator, by contrast, earns exactly $0 on the 360 days a year there's no outage — and costs you maintenance regardless.

This math only works with time-of-use or demand-charge rate structures. On flat-rate billing (~$0.15/kWh all day), there's no spread to capture and the battery is pure backup insurance.

## The Runtime Math Nobody Does

Batteries don't make energy — they store it. When it's gone, it's gone until the grid (or solar) refills it. So do the runtime math before you buy.

**One Tesla Powerwall 3 (13.5 kWh), typical draws:**

| Load scenario | Approx. draw | Runtime |
|---|---|---|
| Whole home, normal use (AC cycling) | 4–6 kW | 2.5–3.5 hours |
| Essentials (fridge, lights, fans, router, phone) | 0.5–1 kW | 13–20 hours |
| Essentials + gas furnace blower (winter) | 1–1.5 kW | 9–13 hours |
| Well pump cycling + fridge + lights | 2–3 kW avg | 5–7 hours |

**A generator, by contrast:** a 7,500W portable on a full tank runs 8–14 hours at half load — and you can refill it. A standby on natural gas runs indefinitely. For a multi-day outage, generators win on runtime, no contest. Batteries win on everything else about the experience.

### The "second battery" trap

Here's where battery quotes get people. The first battery ($9,000–$11,000 installed, $6,300–$7,700 after the 30% credit) covers essentials beautifully. But the moment you want the central AC or the electric range backed up, you're adding a second unit — another $9,000–$11,000. Two batteries after credit: roughly $13,000–$15,000 net, and you *still* have finite runtime.

Compare: a 22 kW standby installed at $12,000–$14,000 runs the entire house indefinitely on natural gas. If whole-home coverage is the goal and you have a gas line, the standby usually wins on economics. Batteries win when your goal is *essentials, silently, with no maintenance* — which, honestly, is what most outages actually require.

The hybrid answer is worth knowing: **battery + solar** changes the equation. With enough solar, a battery recharges daily and can ride through extended outages on essentials — effectively unlimited runtime for critical loads in sunny weather. Without solar, a battery is a finite tank.

## Solar Pairing: Where Batteries Shine

If you already have solar — or are considering it — batteries make more sense than generators in a specific way: solar panels without a battery shut down during outages (grid-tied inverters are required to disconnect for utility worker safety). A battery lets you actually use your panels when the grid is down. For solar homes in areas with time-of-use rates, the battery also earns its keep daily by storing cheap midday power for expensive evening hours — something a generator can never do. Daily payback plus outage backup is the strongest battery case there is.

## Where Batteries Genuinely Win

- **Short outages** (a few hours): silent, instant, no fuel, no going outside in a storm.
- **Noise restrictions:** HOAs, close neighbors, local noise ordinances. Batteries are silent. This alone decides it for some homeowners.
- **No maintenance:** no oil changes, no fuel stabilizer, no exercise schedule, no dead starting battery. (See what generator owners deal with in the [generator maintenance checklist](/articles/generator-maintenance-checklist).)
- **No emissions on-site:** no exhaust, no carbon monoxide risk, no fuel storage.
- **Solar homes:** unlock your panels during outages + daily rate arbitrage.
- **Electronics:** pure sine-wave output, cleaner than utility power. Zero THD concerns.

## Where Generators Genuinely Win

- **Multi-day outages:** a standby on natural gas doesn't run out. A portable refuels from a gas can. A battery is a countdown timer.
- **High-draw loads:** central AC (3–5 kW running, much higher starting surge), electric water heaters, well pumps, electric ranges. These chew through battery capacity fast and can exceed a single battery's output limit.
- **Upfront cost for whole-home:** a $1,200 portable plus a transfer switch covers more raw wattage than a $10,000 battery. If your outage pattern is "once a year for a day," the generator is cheaper by a wide margin.
- **No sun needed:** batteries without solar recharge only when the grid returns. A generator is self-sufficient as long as you have fuel.

## A worked example: one house, one 24-hour outage, both ways

Take the average home from the runtime table above, running essentials plus a gas furnace blower — call it a steady 1–1.5 kW draw.

**The battery house:** one 13.5 kWh unit starts full and carries the house silently for roughly 9–13 hours. By hour 14 the battery is done, and there is no refill — the fridge starts warming, the blower stops, and the house waits for the grid like it has no backup at all. Total spend: $9,000–$11,000 installed, minus the 30% credit.

**The generator house:** the 7,500W portable in the garage runs the same loads at a fraction of its capacity for 8–14 hours per tank, and a trip to the gas can resets the clock. It is louder, smellier, and somebody has to go outside in the weather twice a day — but hour 24 looks exactly like hour 2. Total spend: around $1,500–$3,000 all-in.

Neither result is a trick: the battery won the first half of the outage on experience and lost the second half on physics. Your outage history — not the brochure — tells you which half matters.

## The Honest Verdict Framework

Answer these three questions:

1. **How long are your outages, typically?** Under 6 hours → battery handles it comfortably. Over 24 hours → generator, unless you have solar + multiple batteries.
2. **Do you have (or want) solar?** Yes → battery is the natural pairing. No → generator is simpler and cheaper.
3. **What's the dealbreaker — noise/maintenance, or cost?** If you hate the idea of maintaining an engine and storing gasoline, pay the battery premium. If you want maximum watts per dollar, buy the generator.

The "both" answer is real, by the way: a battery for the instant, silent, short outages (which are most outages) plus a portable generator for the rare multi-day event is a combination I see more homeowners land on. The battery handles 90% of outages beautifully; the generator sits in the garage for the hurricane week.
