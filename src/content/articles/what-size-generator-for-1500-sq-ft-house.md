If you're asking what size generator for a 1500 sq ft house, here's the short answer I give on service calls: **7,500–10,000 watts covers a comfortable outage for most homes this size, and a 14–18kW standby covers everything, central AC included.** The honest longer answer is that square footage doesn't plug into anything. Your AC tonnage and whether you heat with gas or electricity decide this — the floor plan just gives us a good guess at both.

A 1,500 sq ft house is actually the easiest size to get right, because the load profile is predictable. It's also the size where I see the most money wasted, because it's exactly the house dealers love to hang a 22kW unit on. Let's do the math properly.

## Why doesn't square footage size a generator by itself?

Because two 1,500 sq ft houses can differ by 8,000 watts of real load. A 1978 ranch in Ohio with a gas furnace, gas water heater, and a 2.5-ton AC is a modest load. A 2015 build in Florida with a heat pump, electric backup heat strips, an electric range, and a 3-ton AC is a different animal entirely. Same square footage, wildly different generators.

What square footage *does* predict reliably is AC size, and AC is the single biggest sizing factor in most homes. A 1,500 sq ft house in a moderate climate typically runs a **2.5 to 3-ton** central AC. Hot-climate builds (Phoenix, Houston) sometimes push to 3.5 tons; well-insulated northern homes sometimes get away with 2 tons. That tonnage range is where we start.

## What does a typical 1500 sq ft house actually draw?

Here's the realistic load inventory for a home this size. These are numbers I've measured with a clamp meter on installs, not nameplate fantasies — nameplates list maximums, and appliances rarely run at maximum all at once.

| Load | Running watts | Starting watts | Notes for 1,500 sq ft |
|---|---|---|---|
| Central AC, 2.5-ton | 2,400–2,900 | 8,000–10,000 | Common in northern/mixed climates |
| Central AC, 3-ton | 3,000–3,500 | 9,000–12,000 | The most common size for this house |
| Gas furnace blower | 500–800 | 1,000–1,400 | ECM blowers sit at the low end |
| Heat pump (heating) | 3,000–4,000 | 5,000–7,000 | Plus 5,000–10,000W if aux strips kick in |
| Refrigerator | 150–250 | 800–1,200 | Runs about 40% of the time |
| Sump pump (1/2 HP) | 800–1,050 | 2,000–2,150 | Only if you have a basement |
| Well pump (1/2 HP, 240V) | 1,000–1,500 | 3,000–4,000 | Only on well water |
| Lights (LED, whole house) | 200–400 | — | Nobody lights every room at once |
| TV, Wi-Fi, laptops, chargers | 200–400 | — | The "life feels normal" loads |
| Microwave | 1,000–1,500 | 1,500 | Used in bursts, easy to schedule |
| Electric water heater (4,500W element) | 4,500 | 4,500 | The hidden budget-breaker |
| Electric range/oven | 3,000–8,000 | — | Rarely run during outages by choice |

Notice what's *not* huge: everything except the AC, the electric water heater, and electric heat. A 1,500 sq ft home usually has one bathroom-plus, a modest kitchen, and no second AC zone. The base load of a house this size — fridge, lights, electronics, blower — sits around **1,200–2,000 watts** most of the time. That's why the tiers below shake out the way they do. For the full walk-through of adding loads this way on any house, see [What Size Generator Do I Need to Run My House?](/articles/what-size-generator-run-house) — this article is that method applied to one specific size.

## Tier 1: What will 3,500–5,500 watts run?

This is the essentials tier, and for a 1,500 sq ft house it's a genuinely good life during an outage: fridge cold, furnace blower moving warm air (if you heat with gas), sump pump protecting the basement, lights on, phones charged, internet up, TV on. That's 90% of what people actually suffer without.

A 5,000-watt portable or a quiet 4,500-watt inverter handles this tier without breaking a sweat, because the running total hovers near 2,000–2,800 watts and the biggest surge — the sump or well pump — is manageable. What you give up: central AC (window unit in the bedroom is the classic workaround, at 500–800 watts), the electric water heater, the dryer, and cooking on more than one burner at a time.

My honest take: if your outages are short (under 12 hours) and your climate is mild, this tier is the smart money. The generator costs **$500–900**, sips fuel, and stores in a corner. Don't let anyone talk you out of it because it isn't "whole house."

## Tier 2: What does 7,500–10,000 watts get you?

This is the comfort tier, and it's the sweet spot for most 1,500 sq ft homes. Everything in Tier 1, plus:

- **Real cooling** — either two window units, or the central AC with a soft starter installed (more on that below)
- **The microwave and a burner** without playing load Tetris
- **The well pump and sump pump** cycling whenever they want, simultaneously
- **A space heater or two** in winter instead of just the furnace, if you're on portable backup

The running load of a comfortable outage in this house size is typically 3,500–5,500 watts with the AC running — well inside a 9,000-watt portable's continuous rating, with surge headroom left over. A dual-fuel 9,000–10,000-watt unit runs **$900–1,400**, and on propane it'll go 8–10 hours at half load on a 40 lb tank setup. This is the configuration I recommend most often for this house size, and it's not close.

## Tier 3: When do you actually need 14,000–18,000 watts?

When the requirement is "the outage is a non-event and nobody manages anything." A 14–18kW automatic standby with a whole-panel transfer switch runs the 3-ton AC without a soft starter, the electric water heater, the dryer, the range — everything, with standard load-shedding modules handling the rare moment when too much starts at once.

For a 1,500 sq ft house, **14kW is the right standby size in gas-heat homes, and 18kW if you have an electric water heater, electric range, or a heat pump with aux strips.** Installed, you're looking at roughly **$11,000–14,500** for the 14kW and **$12,500–16,000** for the 18kW in most markets — unit, pad, transfer switch, gas line, permits, and my labor. The full installed-cost breakdown lives in [Whole House Generator Cost: What You'll Actually Pay](/articles/whole-house-generator-cost).

Above 18kW in a house this size, you're buying capacity you'll never use. Which brings us to the sales problem.

## Why do dealers push 22kW on a 1500 sq ft house?

Because the math works for them, not you. The unit itself isn't dramatically more expensive — maybe $800–1,500 more wholesale than a 14kW — but the ticket is bigger, the commission is bigger, and "you'll never have to worry" is an easy pitch to a nervous homeowner sitting in the dark after a storm.

What they don't mention: standby generators are happiest at **50–75% load**. A 22kW loafing along at 5kW of load in your 1,500 sq ft house runs cool and dirty. On gasoline-engine standbys (which is what residential units are — air-cooled V-twins, usually), chronic light loading means incomplete combustion, carbon fouling, and oil dilution over time. It also burns roughly **2.4–2.8 gallons of propane per hour** at the loads a house this size actually draws, versus about 1.8–2.2 for a 14kW doing the same job. Across a three-day outage, that's a difference of around 40 gallons — which can be the difference between your 250-gallon tank lasting the outage and not.

An oversized generator is not a safety margin. It's a recurring fuel bill and a maintenance headache wearing a safety margin's clothes. If a quote for your 1,500 sq ft house says 22kW and the dealer can't show you a load calculation that justifies it, get a second quote. There's a deeper dive on what oversizing and undersizing do to AC starting in [What Size Generator Do I Need to Run Central AC?](/articles/what-size-generator-central-ac) — the surge physics are the same whatever the house size.

## A worked example: the house I see most often

Let me run a real one. Three-bedroom 1,500 sq ft ranch, Midwest, built 1998: gas furnace, gas water heater, 3-ton AC, sump pump, city water, gas range. The family wants the AC in summer outages and no load management beyond common sense.

Running loads during a summer outage: AC 3,200W, furnace blower (for the air handler) 600W, fridge 200W, sump pump 900W when cycling, lights and electronics 400W. Running total ≈ **5,300W**. The problem is never the total — it's the AC's startup surge of ~10,500W landing on top of ~2,100W of everything else. Peak demand ≈ **12,600W for a split second**.

Two ways to cover it:

1. **9,000–10,000W dual-fuel portable + soft starter ($300–450 installed).** The soft starter drops that AC surge to roughly 3,500–4,000W. New peak ≈ 6,100W. The portable swallows it easily, and the family keeps about $10,000 in their pocket versus a standby. They wheel it out and plug into a 50-amp inlet with an interlock kit when the power fails.
2. **14kW standby, no soft starter needed.** Peak capacity covers the raw surge with room to spare, it starts itself in 10 seconds, and nobody touches anything. Costs the $10,000 more.

Same house, same loads, same comfortable outage. The difference is purely automation and convenience — which is a perfectly good reason to buy a standby, as long as that's the reason you're paying for.

## Does your heat type change the answer?

Yes, and it's the branch people miss. A gas furnace needs only its blower — 500–800 watts — to heat the whole house. That's why gas-heat 1,500 sq ft homes are cheap to back up.

If you heat with a **heat pump**, winter changes everything. The heat pump itself draws 3,000–4,000 watts, and when outdoor temps drop below roughly 25–35°F, the **auxiliary electric heat strips (5,000–10,000W)** kick in. A portable that cruised through summer outages will buckle in a January ice storm. Heat-pump homes this size either step up to a 12,000W+ portable (and accept that aux heat gets locked out or managed), or go standby at 18kW with load shedding on the strips. If you have **electric baseboard or a full electric furnace**, a portable is essentially off the table for whole-house heat — back up one or two rooms, or go standby.

## Portable or standby for a 1500 sq ft house?

Decide on these three questions, in order:

1. **How long and how often are your outages?** Two short outages a year → portable, Tier 1 or 2, done. Multi-day outages every year, or a house that freezes fast → standby starts paying for itself.
2. **Is anyone home who can't set up a portable?** Standbys exist for medical equipment, elderly parents, and houses that sit empty. If someone in the home depends on powered medical gear, skip to Tier 3 and don't apologize for it.
3. **Do you need the AC running unattended?** Cooling for pets, a server closet, or a wine cellar while you're away means automatic — standby.

## My recommendation for most 1500 sq ft homes

If you forced me to spec one setup for the average 1,500 sq ft gas-heat house, it's a **9,000-watt dual-fuel portable, a 50-amp inlet box with an interlock, and a soft starter on the AC** — about $2,200–2,900 all-in, installed, and it covers everything except the dryer and simultaneous oven use. If the house is all-electric or the family needs automatic coverage, it's a **14kW standby (18kW for heat pumps)** — not a 22kW, no matter what the first quote says.

Whatever you buy, hold the sizing rule I use on every job: add up your realistic running loads, make sure your worst startup surge fits, then keep **20–25% headroom** and stop. Headroom is wisdom. Doubling is waste.
