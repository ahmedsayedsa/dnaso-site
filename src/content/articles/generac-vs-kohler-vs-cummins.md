If you're shopping for a whole-house standby generator, you're going to run into the same four names everywhere: Generac, Kohler, Cummins, and Briggs & Stratton. Dealers all sound confident about whichever brand they sell. Online reviews are a mix of happy owners, furious owners, and people arguing about things that barely matter.

Here's an honest breakdown of what each brand is actually known for — the warranty terms in writing, the real price positioning, how big the dealer network is, and what electricians and long-term owners tend to report. No brand pays me, no brand gave me a test unit, and nobody got a trophy for "best." Picking a standby generator is a $10,000–$16,000 decision; it deserves better than a sales brochure.

## The short version for impatient readers

- **Generac** is the biggest name in the game — the largest dealer network, the most models, and usually the lowest price for a given wattage. Quality reputation is mixed: the high-volume lines have more reported issues, and parts are easy to find because everyone stocks them.
- **Kohler** is the premium pick among the big four — consistently reported as quieter and better finished, with a strong 5-year warranty. It costs more, the dealer network is smaller, and parts can take longer to get.
- **Cummins** is the diesel-industry giant that also sells residential gas standbys. Owners and techs report them as overbuilt and reliable, but the residential lineup is thinner and fewer dealers install them.
- **Briggs & Stratton** sits in the value slot — cheaper than Generac on some models, but with a smaller dealer network and a reputation that varies a lot by model line. Worth a look if your local dealer is strong.

Now the detail.

## Brand-by-brand breakdown

### Generac — the market leader

Generac is the 800-pound gorilla. They sell more residential standby generators in the US than everyone else combined, roughly speaking. That's not a quality claim — it's a market-share fact, and it matters for one practical reason: **any electrician in America has worked on a Generac, and every supply house stocks Generac parts.** When your generator throws a fault code during a storm week in Florida, that matters more than most spec sheets.

**The lineup:** Generac's residential standby line runs from about 10 kW up to 26 kW in air-cooled models (the Guardian series, which is what most homes get), then liquid-cooled units from 22 kW up past 60 kW for big houses. The 22 kW Guardian is the single most-installed home standby in the country.

**Price positioning:** Generac is typically the price leader. A 22 kW Guardian unit alone (before installation) typically runs $5,500–$7,000 depending on the year and region. Installed — with a transfer switch, pad, gas hookup, permits, and a licensed electrician — a 22 kW Generac usually lands in the **$10,000–$14,000** range total. Smaller 14 kW and 18 kW units install for roughly $8,000–$12,000.

**Warranty:** 5 years or 2,000 hours, whichever comes first, on the Guardian residential line — and that's a limited warranty, not bumper-to-bumper. It covers the engine and alternator; the battery, which you'll replace every 2–3 years anyway, isn't covered. Generac also sells an extended warranty (7 years) in some markets through dealers. Read the actual warranty PDF before you sign — "limited" means some components are prorated or excluded, and the warranty requires the unit to be installed by a certified dealer and maintained on schedule.

**Noise:** Generac quotes around 67 dB(A) at 23 feet for the 22 kW Guardian at normal operation. In practice, owners describe it as "a loud lawnmower in a box." It's not silent. It passes most HOA and municipal noise ordinances, which typically set limits around 70–75 dB at the property line, but if the unit sits near a bedroom window or a neighbor's patio, you'll want to think about placement and possibly an acoustic enclosure.

**Reliability reputation:** This is where Generac gets divisive. Because they sell so many units, you hear more complaints in absolute numbers — but you also hear from more happy owners than any other brand. Electricians' common complaints center on the smaller/older lines: controller board issues, stepper-motor (throttle) failures, and a pattern where units that sat unmaintained for years fail at the worst moment. Generac's own weekly self-test helps here — if the battery dies or an oil issue develops, you get a warning before the storm. The recurring advice from service techs: **the maintenance schedule is not optional on a Generac.** Oil, filter, air filter, spark plugs, and battery on time, every time.

**Dealer network:** The biggest by far — thousands of certified dealers and service contractors nationwide. This is Generac's real moat. Buying a standby from anyone, though, means you're married to your *dealer* more than the brand. A great dealer with a mediocre brand beats a great brand with a ghost dealer every time.

### Kohler — the premium standard

Kohler's power division has been building generators for a century — they started with engines in the 1920s, and the residential standby line is a mature, well-engineered product. Among the big four, Kohler carries the strongest "buy it once" reputation among electricians.

**The lineup:** Kohler's residential standby generators run roughly 10 kW to 26 kW in air-cooled models (the 14RCA is the popular middle unit), with liquid-cooled options above that. Fewer SKUs than Generac, which honestly makes choosing simpler.

**Price positioning:** Kohler typically runs **10–20% more than a comparable Generac** — expect installed costs of roughly $11,000–$16,000 for a 14–20 kW setup. The premium is in the engine and the enclosure; Kohler uses a commercial-grade engine design and a corrosion-resistant aluminum enclosure (vs. steel on most Generac units), which matters in coastal salt-air environments.

**Warranty:** 5 years or 2,000 hours limited warranty on the residential line, comparable to Generac's headline terms. Kohler also offers an extended 10-year warranty option on some models through dealers. As with all these warranties: dealer-installed, maintained-on-schedule, and read the exclusions. The battery is a consumable everywhere.

**Noise:** Kohler is consistently reported as the quietest of the big four in the air-cooled class — their 14RCA is rated around 62 dB(A) at 23 feet, which is meaningfully quieter than Generac's 22 kW at 67 dB. Five decibels doesn't sound like much on paper, but dB is logarithmic: 5 dB less is roughly a third less perceived loudness. If the generator sits anywhere near outdoor living space, Kohler's noise advantage is real.

**Reliability reputation:** Among service techs, Kohler gets the fewest complaints per unit sold — though the "per unit" qualifier matters, since they sell far fewer. Common praise: engines that hold up, controllers that don't eat batteries, enclosures that don't rust out in five years. Common complaint: when something *does* break, parts take longer and cost more, because fewer suppliers stock Kohler inventory. Lead time on a Kohler control board during hurricane season is not the same as a Generac board you can get same-day.

**Dealer network:** Smaller than Generac's — significantly. In metro areas you'll find Kohler dealers fine; in rural areas, your "local Kohler dealer" might be a two-hour drive. Verify service coverage before you buy. A Kohler with no nearby service tech is a bad purchase.

### Cummins — the overbuilt option

Cummins is best known for diesel engines in trucks and industrial equipment — the 6.7L in Ram heavy-duty pickups, locomotive engines, power plants. Their residential standby line is a smaller slice of a huge company, and that's both the appeal and the caveat.

**The lineup:** Cummins QuietConnect home standby generators run roughly 13 kW to 20 kW in air-cooled models. The residential lineup is thinner than Generac's or Kohler's — fewer sizes, fewer options. Cummins also has a huge commercial/industrial line, but that's a different purchase conversation.

**Price positioning:** Typically **in the Kohler range or slightly above** — installed 13–20 kW setups usually land around $11,000–$16,000. You're not saving money with Cummins; you're buying the engineering reputation.

**Warranty:** 5 years or 2,000 hours limited on the residential line, similar headline terms to the others. Same advice applies: dealer installation, documented maintenance, read the exclusions.

**Noise:** Cummins QuietConnect units are rated in the low-to-mid 60s dB(A) at 23 feet — comparable to Kohler, quieter than most Generacs in the same class. Owners and installers describe them as noticeably subdued, which is partly why the line is literally called QuietConnect.

**Reliability reputation:** This is Cummins's selling point. The company builds engines that run 500,000 miles in trucks; the residential generator engines are widely described by techs as overbuilt for the job. Failure reports on the home standby line are comparatively rare. The flip side: the product line has changed names and configurations over the years (it was previously sold under the Cummins Onan branding in some channels), which confuses parts lookups for older units. And the thinner lineup means if you need, say, a 26 kW air-cooled unit, Cummins may not have what Generac stocks on the shelf.

**Dealer network:** The smallest of the four for residential. Cummins has enormous commercial service infrastructure, but *residential standby* dealers are sparse — and a commercial diesel tech is not automatically the person you want servicing your 18 kW home unit. Check specifically for residential-install experience before committing.

### Briggs & Stratton — the value play

Briggs & Stratton is the engine brand every American who grew up with a lawnmower knows. Their standby generator line is real and established, but it's the least-discussed of the four — and discussion matters when you're buying something that needs service for 15–20 years.

**The lineup:** Briggs & Stratton home standby generators run roughly 10 kW to 26 kW in air-cooled models, with some liquid-cooled options. The PowerProtect series is their current residential flagship line.

**Price positioning:** Typically **at or below Generac** — the value slot. Installed 20 kW setups commonly land in the $9,000–$13,000 range. If a dealer quotes you a Briggs unit significantly cheaper than Generac for the same wattage, that's normal, not a red flag by itself.

**Warranty:** This is where Briggs has been aggressive — their PowerProtect line has carried a **10-year limited warranty** offer in recent years (terms and availability vary; confirm with the dealer what's actually in writing for the model you're buying). A 10-year headline warranty on a value-priced unit is a strong pitch. As always: dealer-installed, maintenance schedule followed, exclusions read. Long warranties on value lines sometimes carry more exclusions — check what's actually covered in years 6–10 versus years 1–5.

**Noise:** Mid-pack — typically rated in the upper 60s dB(A) at 23 feet for the larger air-cooled units, roughly comparable to Generac. Not the quiet pick.

**Reliability reputation:** The most model-dependent of the four. The newer PowerProtect line gets decent reports from owners and techs; older and cheaper Briggs standby lines had a rockier reputation, particularly around controller electronics. Because Briggs sells fewer standbys, the owner-report sample size is smaller, which cuts both ways — fewer complaints filed, but less track record to judge. One consistent positive: Briggs engines themselves are a known quantity, and parts for the engine side are widely available.

**Dealer network:** Smaller than Generac's and roughly comparable to Kohler's, but with thinner density in some regions. Same rule: verify your actual local service situation, not the brand's national dealer-count map.

## The things that matter more than the brand

Honest talk: once you're comparing the big four at the same wattage, the brand is maybe the fourth-most-important decision. Here's what actually determines whether you'll be happy:

**1. The installer.** A standby generator is not a plug-in appliance. It needs a concrete pad or composite base, a gas line sized for the load (a 22 kW unit at full tilt burns roughly 3.5–3.6 gallons of propane per hour, or about 280–290 cubic feet of natural gas per hour), a transfer switch wired into your panel, permits, and often a gas-meter upgrade. A sloppy install — undersized gas line is the classic — will make any brand run badly. Get three quotes. Ask each installer how many standbys they did last year, who services what they sell, and what their emergency response time is during an outage.

**2. The maintenance contract.** Every one of these warranties requires maintenance on schedule: oil and filter annually or every 100–200 hours, air filter, spark plugs every couple of years, battery every 2–3 years, valve adjustment every few years on some engines. Budget **$300–$500/year** for a dealer maintenance plan. Owners who skip maintenance are the single biggest source of "this brand is junk" reviews across all four brands.

**3. Fuel type and availability.** Natural gas is the set-and-forget choice — unlimited runtime as long as the gas flows (during most storms it does, but not always; the 2021 Texas freeze disrupted gas in places). Propane means a tank you own and refill: a 22 kW unit burns about 3.5 gallons/hour at full load, so a 500-gallon tank (filled to 80%, i.e., 400 usable gallons) gives you roughly 100+ hours at full load, longer at partial load. Size the tank for your realistic outage scenario, not the brochure's.

**4. Sizing.** Don't buy 26 kW because the number feels safe. A proper load calculation — or at minimum, a careful inventory of what you need to run — usually lands most homes at 14–22 kW. Oversizing wastes fuel and money; undersizing means the generator sheds loads or stalls when the AC kicks on.

## Verdict: which brand for which buyer

**Buy Generac if:** you want the lowest price for a given wattage, the fastest parts availability, and the easiest time finding a service tech anywhere in the country — and you're committed to the maintenance schedule. Best for: budget-conscious buyers, rural areas where dealer choice is thin, and anyone who values a deep parts and knowledge base.

**Buy Kohler if:** noise matters (unit near bedrooms, patio, or close neighbors), you're in a coastal or corrosive environment (aluminum enclosure), and you have a solid local Kohler dealer. Best for: buyers who'll pay 10–20% more for a quieter, better-finished machine and can verify local service.

**Buy Cummins if:** you want the most overbuilt engine in the class and the quietest operation, and you've confirmed a dealer with real residential-install experience in your area. Best for: buyers who keep equipment for decades and prioritize mechanical robustness over selection breadth.

**Buy Briggs & Stratton if:** the price is right from a dealer you trust, the model carries the long warranty in writing, and you've verified local service. Best for: value buyers with a strong local dealer — the brand matters less than the dealer relationship here.

**The one-line version:** pick your installer first, your wattage second, your fuel third — then buy whichever of the four your chosen installer sells, services, and stocks parts for. That decision will serve you better than any brand debate on the internet.
