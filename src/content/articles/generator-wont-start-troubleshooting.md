The power goes out, you wheel out the generator, pull the cord three times, and... nothing. Or you get the alert from your standby unit that it failed its weekly exercise cycle. Either way, that sinking feeling is the same.

Here's the good news: roughly 80% of no-start problems come down to a short list of simple causes, and most of them can be checked and fixed in 15 minutes with basic hand tools. The trick is working in the right order, from most likely to least likely, so you're not chasing your tail.

Before you touch anything, three safety rules:

1. **Disconnect the spark plug wire** before doing any inspection or repair beyond cranking checks. You do not want the engine firing while your fingers are near moving parts.
2. **Work on a cool engine.** Generators get extremely hot. If you just tried to start it repeatedly, give it 10–15 minutes to cool.
3. **Handle fuel carefully.** Work outdoors or in a ventilated garage with the door open. No smoking, no open flames, and wipe up any spills immediately.

With that covered, let's go in order.

## Step 1: Check the Fuel (The #1 Cause)

If your generator sat for months and now won't start, the fuel is your prime suspect. Regular pump gasoline starts going stale in **3 to 6 months** without a fuel stabilizer. What "stale" actually means: the lighter, more volatile compounds evaporate off, and the remaining fuel oxidizes and forms varnish-like deposits that clog the carburetor's tiny passages. By the time the gas smells like varnish or turpentine instead of fresh gasoline, it's done.

**What to check:**

- **Is there fuel in the tank at all?** Sounds obvious, but fuel gauges on portables are notoriously optimistic, and fuel can evaporate through vented caps over a long season. Open the cap and look inside. On a typical 5,000-watt portable with a 6–7 gallon tank, you need at least a couple of gallons sloshing around — a cupful in the bottom won't reach the pickup.
- **How old is the fuel?** Be honest. If you can't remember filling it, or you filled it "last summer" and it's now winter, assume it's bad. Stabilized fuel with a product like STA-BIL lasts **12–24 months** depending on the formula; unstabilized fuel does not.
- **Smell it.** Fresh gasoline has a sharp, clean chemical smell. Stale gas smells sour, varnish-like, or like old paint. If it smells wrong, don't try to run the engine on it.
- **Look for water contamination.** Ethanol-blended gasoline (E10, which is what 95% of US stations sell) absorbs moisture from the air. Enough absorbed water and the ethanol separates out — this is called **phase separation** — leaving a layer of water-ethanol mix at the bottom of the tank, right where the fuel pickup sits. The engine gets a gulp of water and dies or won't start at all.

**The fix:** If the fuel is old or questionable, drain the tank completely (use a hand siphon pump into an approved gas can — never mouth-siphon). Drain the carburetor bowl too; there's usually a small drain screw or bolt at the bottom of the float bowl. Refill with fresh gasoline, and this time add fuel stabilizer immediately. If the engine runs fine on fresh fuel, the problem is solved — and the carburetor hadn't yet gummed up permanently.

**Cost:** $0 if you have a siphon and fresh gas on hand. A quart of stabilizer runs about $8–12 and treats 40–80 gallons, so there's no excuse to skip it.

**Prevention going forward:** Add stabilizer to every fill-up, not just before storage. Running the generator dry before storage (shutting the fuel valve and letting it run until it dies) is even better, though on some EFI and standby units this isn't recommended — check your manual.

## Step 2: Check the Oil Level

This one surprises people, but it's one of the most common causes on newer portables. Most modern generators have a **low-oil shutdown sensor** that kills the spark if the oil level drops below a minimum threshold. The engine will crank, maybe cough once or twice, and then nothing — or it will start and die after a few seconds. The behavior mimics a fuel problem, so people rebuild carburetors when all they needed was half a quart of oil.

**What to check:**

- Pull the dipstick (or remove the fill cap if there's no dipstick — common on smaller inverter generators like the Honda EU2200i). On most generators, the oil level should be at or near the **top of the fill neck** when the unit is sitting level.
- Check the oil condition too. Black, sludgy oil that smells like gasoline suggests fuel has diluted the crankcase oil — usually from a carburetor float valve leaking fuel past the rings during storage. That needs an oil change and a fuel valve shutoff habit.
- **Don't trust the slope.** If you pulled the generator out of the shed and it's sitting at an angle on grass, the sensor can read low even with adequate oil. Level the unit first.

**The fix:** Top off with the oil weight your manual specifies — typically **SAE 10W-30** for most climates, 5W-30 for cold weather starts below freezing. A quart of conventional 10W-30 costs about $5–7; a small engine usually takes 0.6 to 1.1 quarts (check your manual — a Generac GP6500 takes about 1.06 qt, a Honda EU2200i takes 0.42 qt).

**One important exception:** Some very cheap or very old generators don't have low-oil shutdown. If yours doesn't and the oil is genuinely empty, **do not run it** — running a small engine with no oil will seize it in minutes, and that's a new-engine problem, not a repair problem.

## Step 3: Check the Battery (Electric-Start Portables and Standby Units)

Recoil-start portables don't have batteries, so skip this step if yours has only a pull cord. But any generator with an electric start button or remote start — and every automatic standby unit — depends on a battery, and batteries are consumable items.

**Electric-start portable generators:**

- Turn the key or press the start button. If you hear nothing at all, or a slow labored crank that fades, the battery is dead or dying.
- Portable generator batteries are small 12V lead-acid units (typically 12–35 Ah). They self-discharge in storage, and heat kills them faster. Typical lifespan: **2 to 4 years**.
- Check the terminals for corrosion — white or green crusty buildup. Clean with a wire brush and apply dielectric grease or petroleum jelly.
- Charge the battery with a small charger (a 2-amp trickle charger works; expect 6–12 hours for a full charge). If it won't hold a charge after a full charging cycle, replace it. Replacement batteries run **$30–60** depending on the model.

**Standby generators (Generac, Kohler, Cummins):**

This is the single most common standby no-start cause. The standby's battery sits on a constant trickle charge from the unit's built-in charger, but:

- Batteries still age out. Standby batteries typically last **2 to 3 years**, sometimes 4 in mild climates.
- A battery that's been cycled hard during long outages degrades faster.
- Check the controller display first — most standby controllers (Generac Evolution, Kohler RDC2) will show a battery warning or a failed-exercise alarm with a fault code. Write down the exact code; the manual's fault table will tell you if it's battery, RPM sense, overspeed, or something else.

**Testing a battery properly:** A voltmeter reading of 12.6V on a disconnected battery looks fine, but voltage at rest doesn't tell you if the battery can deliver cranking amps. Many standby controllers run a basic self-test, but a real **load test** (available at any auto parts store, usually free) is the honest answer. A battery that reads 12.4V and collapses to 8V under load is dead.

**Replacement cost:** Standby batteries (typically Group 26R or U1 size) run **$80–150**. Replacing one is usually a 10-minute job with a wrench — disconnect negative first, then positive; reverse the order on install.

## Step 4: Check the Spark Plug

If fuel, oil, and battery are all good, pull the spark plug. This takes five minutes with a spark plug socket and tells you a lot about what's happening inside the engine.

**What to check:**

- **Fouling.** A black, sooty plug suggests a rich condition or a chronically dirty air filter. An oily plug suggests worn rings or valve seals (less common on low-hour generators). A white or blistered plug suggests overheating or a lean condition.
- **Gap.** Check your manual for the spec — commonly **0.028–0.031 inches** for many portable generators. A gap that's drifted wide weakens the spark.
- **Cracks.** Look closely at the ceramic insulator. A hairline crack can short the spark to the shell instead of the tip, giving you an intermittent no-start that's maddening to diagnose.
- **The spark test.** With the plug removed and reconnected to the wire, ground the plug's metal shell against the engine block, crank the engine, and look for a strong blue spark jumping the gap. A weak yellow spark or no spark points to the plug, the ignition coil, or the kill-switch circuit.

**The fix:** Spark plugs are cheap — **$3–8** for the NGK or Champion plug most small engines use. If the plug is fouled or old, just replace it rather than cleaning it; it's not worth gambling a no-start during the next outage on a $5 part. While you're in there, note the plug model number (e.g., NGK BPR6ES is common) and keep a spare in your generator's toolkit.

If the new plug still gives no spark, you're likely looking at a failed ignition coil (roughly **$25–60** for the part, a 20-minute swap) or a stuck/broken kill switch — check that the engine switch is actually in the RUN position and the low-oil sensor wire is connected. A disconnected oil-sensor wire on some models reads as "low oil" and kills the spark permanently.

## Step 5: Check the Choke Position

This sounds trivial, but mis-set chokes cause a huge number of no-starts, especially on cold engines being started by someone who hasn't used the generator in months.

**Cold engine:** The choke should be **closed (ON)** for the first pull or two. The choke restricts air to enrich the fuel mixture, which a cold engine needs. If you crank a cold engine with the choke off, you'll flood it with weak mixture and it won't catch.

**Warm engine:** The choke should be **open (OFF)**. If you try to restart a warm engine with the choke still closed, you'll flood it — too much fuel, not enough air. A flooded engine is easy to spot: strong fuel smell, wet spark plug, and the engine may "chug" without catching.

**Clearing a flood:** Turn the choke fully OFF, hold the throttle wide open if the generator has one, and pull 4–6 times with the fuel valve closed (or just crank a standby briefly). This clears the excess fuel. Alternatively, remove the spark plug, dry it off, pull the starter cord a few times with the plug out to blow fuel vapor out of the cylinder, then reinstall and try again with correct choke.

**Auto-choke units:** Many modern standby generators and some inverter portables use an automatic choke. If the auto-choke solenoid or linkage fails, the engine gets the wrong mixture on start. Symptoms: starts fine in warm weather but not cold, or needs multiple attempts. The solenoid is a small replaceable part (**$15–40**), though diagnosing it requires confirming the choke plate actually moves during cranking — have someone watch the linkage while you crank.

## Step 6: Check the Air Filter

A generator is an air pump that happens to burn fuel. Starve it of air and it won't start or won't run properly under load.

**What to check:** Remove the air filter cover (usually one or two clips or screws). Look at the filter element:

- **Paper/pleated filters** (most portables): hold it up to a light. If you can't see light through the pleats, it's clogged. Tapping it gently can knock loose dust off for an emergency start, but plan to replace it.
- **Foam filters** (many inverter models like the Honda EU series): these are oiled foam. If the foam is saturated with oil, dried out, crumbling, or caked with dust, it's done.
- **Dual-element filters** (larger portables): check both the foam pre-cleaner and the paper element.

**The fix:** Replacement air filters are **$8–20**. Foam filters can sometimes be washed in warm soapy water, dried completely, and re-oiled with clean engine oil (squeeze out the excess — it should be tacky, not dripping). Paper filters can't be cleaned effectively; replace them.

**Check the intake path too.** Mice love building nests in generator air boxes during storage — it's warm, sheltered, and they chew the filter into bedding. If you find nesting material, clean it all out and check that no debris got past the filter into the carburetor throat.

**How often:** Check the air filter every **50 hours** of run time or at the start of each storm season, whichever comes first. In dusty conditions (construction sites, desert areas), check more often.

## Step 7: The Carburetor — Clean It or Replace It

If you've worked through steps 1–6 and the engine still won't start, the carburetor is the next and most likely culprit — especially if the generator sat with unstabilized fuel. Old gas leaves varnish and gum deposits that clog the carburetor's tiny jets and passages. The main jet on a small generator carburetor has an opening roughly the diameter of a human hair; it doesn't take much varnish to block it.

**Symptoms of a gummed carburetor:**

- Engine starts with starter fluid (sprayed into the air intake) but dies immediately — this proves spark and compression are fine and the carburetor isn't delivering fuel.
- Engine starts and runs only with the choke partially closed — the choke is compensating for a clogged main jet.
- Engine surges up and down at no load (hunting), which can indicate a partially clogged jet or a governor issue.
- You drained old, varnish-smelling fuel in Step 1 — whatever was in the tank was also sitting in the carburetor bowl, evaporating and concentrating the gum.

**Option A: Clean it.** Remove the carburetor (usually 2 bolts to the intake manifold plus fuel line and linkage — take a photo before disconnecting anything). Disassemble it on a clean tray: float bowl, float, needle valve, main jet, pilot jet, emulsion tube. Soak metal parts in carburetor cleaner (**$10–15** a can), blow out every passage with compressed air, and poke jets clear with a soft copper wire or jet-cleaning tool — never steel, which enlarges the jet openings. A full cleaning takes **45–90 minutes** for a first-timer. Carburetor rebuild kits with new gaskets and needle run **$10–25**.

**Option B: Replace it.** For most common portable generators, a complete aftermarket carburetor costs **$15–35** on Amazon or at a small-engine shop — often barely more than a rebuild kit, and it arrives clean and ready to bolt on. For a Honda EU2200i, an OEM carburetor runs closer to **$80–120**; aftermarket clones are **$20–30** and generally work, though quality varies. For most people, replacement is the better value: 15 minutes of wrenching versus an hour-plus of fiddly cleaning, and a brand-new carburetor eliminates the "did I get every passage?" doubt.

**Which to choose:** If the carburetor is a common model (Predator 3500/4375, Generac GP series, Westinghouse iGen series), replace it — the aftermarket carbs are cheap and plentiful. If it's an unusual or discontinued model where replacements are hard to find, clean and rebuild the original. If the carburetor is physically damaged (cracked body, stripped threads), replace it.

## Portable vs. Standby: Different Machines, Different Checks

The seven steps above cover both types, but the emphasis shifts depending on what you own.

### Portable generators

| Check | Portable-specific notes |
|---|---|
| Fuel | Stale gas is the #1 portable killer — these sit unused for months |
| Oil | Low-oil sensor kills spark; check on level ground |
| Battery | Only on electric-start models; 2–4 year life |
| Fuel valve | Make sure the fuel petcock/valve is OPEN — commonly left closed after storage |
| Run switch | Confirm the engine switch is on RUN, not STOP |

The **fuel valve** deserves emphasis: after storage, many owners forget they shut the fuel valve when running the generator dry. The engine cranks on the fuel left in the carburetor bowl, runs for 30–60 seconds, and dies. If your generator starts then dies after a minute, check the valve before anything else.

### Standby generators

| Check | Standby-specific notes |
|---|---|
| Controller display | Read the fault code FIRST — it often names the problem directly |
| Battery | #1 standby no-start cause; 2–3 year life despite trickle charging |
| Fuel supply | Natural gas: verify the gas valve at the unit is open and the meter isn't locked. Propane: check tank level — below ~10–15% the vapor pressure may be too low |
| Exercise cycle | A failed weekly exercise is your early warning — don't ignore the alert |
| Transfer switch | If the generator runs but the house has no power, the problem is the transfer switch, not the engine |

Standby units on **natural gas** rarely have fuel-quality problems (pipeline gas doesn't go stale), so your diagnostic order shifts: battery, then controller codes, then fuel supply, then spark/ignition. On **propane** standbys, verify the tank has fuel and the regulator isn't frozen or failed — a regulator that's 10+ years old is a suspect.

**Do not attempt** to service the natural gas or propane plumbing yourself. Gas line work — checking pressures, replacing regulators, touching the meter — belongs to a licensed gas technician or your utility. The engine-side troubleshooting above is fine for a homeowner; anything involving pressurized fuel gas is not.

## When to Call a Professional

There's no shame in stopping. Call a small-engine shop or generator service tech when:

- **You've worked the full checklist and it still won't start.** At that point you're likely looking at low compression (worn rings or valves), a failed ignition module, or internal damage — all of which need specialized tools like a compression tester and leak-down gauge.
- **The engine has no compression.** If the pull cord offers almost no resistance, or a compression test reads below spec (typically **90–120 PSI** minimum for small engines, though it varies by model), the engine needs internal work — valve adjustment at minimum, a rebuild at worst.
- **You smell gas strongly and can't find the leak.** Fuel leaks near a hot engine are a fire hazard. Stop, ventilate, and get help.
- **Anything involving the transfer switch or standby electrical connections.** The transfer switch sits between utility power and your panel — mistakes there can backfeed the grid and endanger lineworkers, or destroy the generator. This is electrician territory.
- **The generator is under warranty.** DIY repairs can void it. Generac residential standbys typically carry a **5-year limited warranty**; most portables carry 2–3 years. Check before you wrench.
- **It's a standby unit with repeated fault codes you can't clear.** Modern controllers log faults, and a tech with the dealer software can pull history that tells the real story.

**What a service call costs:** Expect **$100–175** for a diagnostic visit in most US markets, with common repairs (carburetor replacement, battery swap, tune-up) landing in the **$150–350** range including parts and labor. A full portable tune-up (oil, plug, filter, carburetor service) typically runs **$150–250**. Standby annual maintenance contracts run **$300–500/year** and include the exercise-cycle monitoring, battery test, oil and filter change, and a full load test.
