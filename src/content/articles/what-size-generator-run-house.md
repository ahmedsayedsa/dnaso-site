This is the number-one question in home backup power, and most people get it wrong in the same way: they buy too small and find out during the outage. The fix takes about ten minutes with a pencil. Here's how an electrician would size it for your house.

## Running watts vs. starting watts — the two numbers that matter

Every motor-driven appliance has two wattage ratings:

- **Running watts:** the steady draw while it operates. Your fridge hums along at 150–200 watts.
- **Starting (surge) watts:** the brief spike when the motor kicks on — typically 2–3x the running watts, lasting a few seconds.

Your generator must satisfy **both** conditions at once:

1. Its **running-watt rating** must cover everything operating simultaneously.
2. Its **starting-watt rating** must cover the biggest surge in your setup.

Miss condition 1 and the generator overloads and trips. Miss condition 2 and the compressor or pump stalls on startup — the generator bogs down, voltage sags, and you get the dreaded brown-out that can damage the appliance motor.

Resistive loads (lights, phone chargers, toasters, space heaters) have no surge — starting watts = running watts. Motors (compressors, pumps, fans) are where the surges live.

## The appliance wattage table

These are typical ranges. Your specific appliance may differ — check the nameplate (the label on the back or bottom) for the most accurate number.

| Appliance | Running watts | Starting watts |
|---|---|---|
| Refrigerator / freezer | 150–200 | 1,000–1,200 |
| Furnace blower (gas/oil heat) | 700–900 | 2,000–2,500 |
| Sump pump (1/3 HP) | 800–900 | 1,800–2,200 |
| Well pump (1/2 HP) | 1,000–1,100 | 2,500–3,000 |
| Central AC, 3-ton | 3,000–3,500 | 9,000–12,000 |
| Central AC, 4-ton | 4,000–4,500 | 11,000–14,000 |
| Central AC, 5-ton | 5,000–5,500 | 13,000–16,000 |
| Window AC (10,000 BTU) | 900–1,000 | 2,200–2,600 |
| Portable AC (12,000 BTU) | 1,100–1,300 | 2,500–3,000 |
| Microwave | 1,000–1,200 | 1,000–1,200 (no surge) |
| Electric range (one burner) | 1,500–2,000 | no surge |
| Electric water heater | 4,000–4,500 | no surge |
| Clothes dryer (electric) | 5,000 | no surge |
| LED lights (per bulb) | 8–12 | no surge |
| TV (55") | 100–150 | no surge |
| Laptop / phone chargers | 50–100 | no surge |
| Wi-Fi router + modem | 20–30 | no surge |
| CPAP machine | 30–60 | no surge |
| Garage door opener | 500–600 | 1,200–1,400 |
| Chest freezer | 150–200 | 1,000–1,200 |

**The pattern to notice:** the big surge loads are the motors — AC compressors, well pumps, sump pumps, furnace blowers. Everything else is straightforward.

## The 5-minute sizing method

Here's the exact process:

1. **List what you want to run** during an outage. Be honest — not "everything," just what matters.
2. **Add up the running watts.**
3. **Find the biggest surge.** Take the appliance with the largest (starting − running) difference, and add that difference to your running total.
4. **Add 20% headroom.** Multiply by 1.2. Generators last longer, run quieter, and handle unexpected loads better when they're not pinned at 100%.

That final number is your target running-watt rating. Check that the generator's starting-watt rating covers your biggest single surge with room to spare.

### Worked example 1: Essentials only (any size home)

Fridge + freezer, some LED lights, Wi-Fi, phone/laptop chargers, and a gas furnace blower for winter heat.

| Load | Running | Surge extra |
|---|---|---|
| Refrigerator | 200 | 1,000 |
| Chest freezer | 180 | 1,000 |
| Furnace blower | 800 | 1,500 |
| 10 LED bulbs | 100 | — |
| Wi-Fi + chargers + laptop | 100 | — |
| **Running total** | **1,380 W** | |

Biggest surge: furnace blower or fridge (1,000–1,500 W extra). Running total + biggest surge = 1,380 + 1,500 = 2,880 W. × 1.2 headroom = **~3,450 W**.

**Answer: a 4,000–5,000 watt portable** (like the Generac GP6500) handles this with room to spare. This is the most common real-world setup — it costs $700–$1,000 and covers the things that actually cost you money when they stop (food, heat, sump pump).

### Worked example 2: Medium home, 2,000 sq ft, no central AC

Add a sump pump, a microwave, and a window AC to the essentials.

| Load | Running | Surge extra |
|---|---|---|
| Essentials (from above) | 1,380 | — |
| Sump pump | 900 | 1,300 |
| Window AC 10k BTU | 1,000 | 1,600 |
| Microwave | 1,200 | — |
| **Running total** | **4,480 W** | |

Biggest surge: window AC (1,600 W extra). 4,480 + 1,600 = 6,080. × 1.2 = **~7,300 W**.

**Answer: a 7,500–9,000 watt portable** — the Champion 100520 (8,750 starting) or Westinghouse WGen7500 class, $800–$1,100. This is the sweet spot for most suburban homes that don't need central AC.

### Worked example 3: 3,000 sq ft home WITH central AC (3-ton)

This is where the math jumps. The AC compressor is the elephant.

| Load | Running | Surge extra |
|---|---|---|
| Refrigerator + freezer | 400 | 1,000 |
| Lights, Wi-Fi, chargers, TV | 400 | — |
| Furnace blower | 800 | 1,500 |
| Sump pump | 900 | 1,300 |
| Central AC, 3-ton | 3,500 | 8,000 |
| **Running total** | **6,000 W** | |

Biggest surge: the AC — 8,000 W on top of its running draw. 6,000 + 8,000 = 14,000. × 1.2 = **~16,800 W running**, with a starting requirement near **17,000–18,000 W**.

**Answer: a 20–22 kW standby generator.** No portable under about 12,000 watts will reliably start a 3-ton central AC — and even then it's marginal. This is exactly why homes with central AC usually end up with a Generac 22kW Guardian or Kohler 20RCA ($8,000–$15,000 installed).

**The cheaper alternative:** install a [soft starter](/articles/what-size-generator-central-ac/) on the AC (~$300–$400) to cut the starting surge by 60–70%. That can drop the surge to ~3,500 W and bring a big portable back into play. Read the AC sizing guide for the full breakdown.

## The "add 20% headroom" rule — and why it exists

Three reasons:

1. **Motors age.** A 10-year-old well pump draws more starting current than it did new.
2. **You forget things.** The garage door opener, the dehumidifier, the second fridge in the garage — something always shows up during the outage that wasn't on the list.
3. **Heat derates generators.** A generator rated 7,500 W at sea level makes less at altitude and in extreme heat. Headroom absorbs that.

## Why oversizing wastes fuel

Go too far the other way and you pay at the fuel pump. A 22kW standby idling at 4kW of load still burns a meaningful amount of fuel per hour — roughly 1.5–2 gallons of propane — while a right-sized 7,500-watt portable at the same load sips about half a gallon of gasoline. Generators are most efficient at 50–80% load. Size to your math plus 20%, not double.

The exception: if you're buying a standby, the price jump from 14kW to 22kW is often only $800–$1,200 installed, and it buys you the ability to run central AC. That's a reasonable oversize because the load (AC) is real and intermittent.

## Common sizing mistakes

- **Counting square footage instead of watts.** A 1,500 sq ft all-electric home needs more generator than a 3,000 sq ft home with gas heat and no central AC. Watts, not square feet.
- **Forgetting the well pump.** Rural homeowners lose water when the power goes out. A 1/2 HP well pump needs 2,500–3,000 starting watts — it belongs on the list.
- **Assuming two big motors start at the same time.** They rarely do. You size for the biggest single surge, not all surges added together — unless you know they'll coincide (like AC and well pump on the same thermostat cycle, which is rare).
- **Trusting the "peak" number on the box.** Some manufacturers advertise the starting watts in big print. Size by running watts for the load, starting watts for the surge. Read both.

## What size generator do most houses need? (quick reference)

- **Essentials only** (fridge, lights, phones, furnace): **4,000–5,500 W** portable
- **Essentials + sump pump + window AC + microwave:** **7,500–9,500 W** portable or inverter
- **Whole house, no central AC:** **10,000–14,000 W** portable or small standby
- **Whole house with 3-ton central AC:** **20–22 kW** standby
- **Whole house with 4–5 ton central AC:** **22–26 kW** standby
