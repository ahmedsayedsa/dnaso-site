Somebody asks me this on almost every outage call: how long will a generator run on 5 gallons of gas? It's usually day two of a storm cleanup, they're looking at one full gas can in the garage, and they want to know if it gets them through tonight. The honest answer is: it depends almost entirely on two numbers — how big the generator is, and how hard you're asking it to work. Five gallons can be an evening. It can also be most of a week of careful, part-time power. Here's how to tell which one you're holding.

## What's the short answer for each generator size?

If you just want the planning numbers, here they are. These assume gasoline, a healthy engine, and steady loads — real-world figures I've seen in the field, cross-checked against manufacturer consumption data:

| Generator class | 25% load | 50% load | 100% load |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2,000W inverter | ~40 hrs (0.12 gph) | ~25 hrs (0.20 gph) | ~16 hrs (0.31 gph) |
| 3,500–5,500W portable | ~16 hrs (0.30 gph) | ~11 hrs (0.45 gph) | ~7 hrs (0.70 gph) |
| 7,500W portable | ~12 hrs (0.40 gph) | ~8 hrs (0.65 gph) | ~5 hrs (1.0 gph) |

One important catch on the small inverter row: a 2,000W inverter's tank only holds about a gallon. "Five gallons" for that machine means the fuel in the tank plus your gas can, with refills along the way — a Honda EU2200i runs about 8 hours per tankful at quarter load and about 3.2 hours per tankful flat-out. The consumption rate is what's tiny; the tank is too. Mid-size and large portables, with 4- to 7-gallon tanks, get much closer to swallowing 5 gallons in one fill.

## How do you work out the runtime for your own generator?

The math is one division problem, and once you've done it once you'll never trust a box label again:

**Runtime (hours) = gallons ÷ gallons-per-hour at your load**

Say your 5,500W generator burns 0.45 gallons per hour at half load. Five gallons ÷ 0.45 = **11.1 hours**. That's it. Every runtime claim in every manual is this same division with a friendlier consumption number plugged in.

Where do you find your consumption number? Three places, in order of honesty:

1. **Your own log.** Fill the tank, run a known load for two hours, and measure what it takes to refill. This is the only number that's truly yours — it accounts for your engine's age, your altitude, and your actual loads.
2. **The manual's fuel-consumption or runtime spec.** If it says "11 hours at 50% load on a 5-gallon tank," that's 5 ÷ 11 ≈ 0.45 gph at half load. Work it backwards like that and the marketing fog clears.
3. **The table above.** Close enough for outage budgeting, which is what most people actually need.

What you can't do is use one load's number for a different load. Consumption isn't linear in a simple way — a conventional generator's engine spins at 3,600 RPM whether it's powering a phone charger or a well pump, so there's a baseline burn just for keeping the engine turning. That's why a big generator at tiny load is so wasteful, and it leads straight into the next point.

## What does 5 gallons look like across one real outage day?

Let me make this concrete with the setup I see most often: a 5,500W portable, a family riding out a storm outage, and one 5-gallon can. Run in blocks, here's a typical day:

- **6–9 a.m. (3 hrs):** Fridge and freezer pull down cold, coffee maker, lights, phones on chargers, a quick well-pump top-up of the pressure tank. Average load around 40–50%. Burn: roughly 1.3 gallons.
- **9 a.m.–5 p.m. (off):** Generator shut down. The fridge coasts — a modern one holds temperature fine for 4 hours unopened, and this house opens it twice, quickly, for lunch fixings. Burn: zero.
- **5–10 p.m. (5 hrs):** The big block. Dinner cooking (electric skillet, not the 8,000-watt range), TV, lights, showers with the pump running, everything rechargeable plugged in, fridge pulling back down. Average load near 50%, spiking higher. Burn: roughly 2.3 gallons.
- **10 p.m.–6 a.m. (off, or inverter duty):** The portable sleeps. If there's a small inverter in the garage, it hums along at 0.15 gph keeping the fridge cycling and a CPAP running — about 1 gallon across the whole night, and quiet enough that the neighbors stay friendly. Without one, the fridge just coasts until morning; a full, unopened fridge survives the night.

Total for the portable: about **3.6 gallons for a full, comfortable day** — which means that one 5-gallon can, run this way, covers a day and a half instead of the 11 straight hours the simple math suggests. That's not a trick; it's the difference between owning a generator and managing one. The customers who run out of fuel on day two are almost never the ones with the smallest cans. They're the ones who never turned the machine off.

## Why do manufacturers' "up to" runtime claims mislead people?

Because that big number on the box — "up to 18 hours of runtime!" — is measured at **25% load**. On a 5,500W generator, 25% load is about 1,375 watts: a fridge, a handful of LED lights, a TV, and some chargers. That's a real outage load for some people, but the moment you add a well pump, a microwave, a space heater, or a window AC, you're at 50% or beyond, and the hours fall off a cliff.

My rule of thumb when a customer reads me a box claim: **at 50% load, expect about two-thirds of the advertised hours. At full load, expect about half.** An "18-hour" generator is an 11- to 12-hour generator at half load and a 7- to 9-hour generator working hard. None of this is a scandal — the test standard is what it is — but you should budget with the derated numbers, not the billboard numbers.

Inverter generators are the honorable exception in one specific way: their engines throttle down at light load instead of holding 3,600 RPM, so their light-load consumption genuinely craters. That's the entire reason the 2,000W inverter row in the table looks almost absurd next to the others. If your outage loads are small and mostly at night, an inverter sipping 0.12 gallons per hour is doing something a conventional machine physically cannot.

## How should you budget 5 gallons across a real outage?

Here's the planning number I give customers with a mid-size portable: **budget about 5 gallons per day.** At mixed real-world loads — fridge cycling, some lights, chargers, the occasional pump run — a 3,500–5,500W machine running most of the day lands right around there. Two days of outage, two cans. Three days, three cans, and you'd better know which gas stations have power, because everyone else in the county did the same math on day one.

What does that cost? At around $3.00–3.50 a gallon, a day of mid-size generator power runs you roughly **$15–18 in fuel** — cheap compared to a fridge full of spoiled food ($200–400, easily) or one burst pipe. Running a 7,500W unit hard all day is a different story: at 0.65–1.0 gph around the clock, you're burning 15–24 gallons a day, or $45–80. Big generators are wonderful for two hours of heavy loads and expensive pets the other twenty-two.

And a word on standby generators, since people ask: whole-house standby units don't really play the 5-gallon game — they run on natural gas or propane. A 22kW standby on propane burns roughly 2 gallons per hour at half load and close to 4 at full load, so a 5-gallon cylinder's worth of propane is gone in about an hour and a half to two and a half hours. That's why standbys get plumbed into 250- or 500-gallon tanks, not grill bottles. ## How do you stretch 5 gallons when it's all you have?

This is where outage experience pays. When fuel is the limit, you manage hours, not watts:

**Duty-cycle the fridge and freezer.** A modern fridge holds safe temperatures for about 4 hours unopened; a full freezer, 24–48 hours. Run the generator in blocks — 1–2 hours on to pull temperatures back down, then off. Most households can keep food safe on 6–8 hours of total generator run time per day instead of 24. That's your 5 gallons lasting two days instead of half of one.

**Let the big machine sleep at night.** Overnight loads are tiny: fridge cycles, a CPAP, phone chargers, maybe a fan. That's inverter-generator work at 0.12–0.2 gph — or battery-station work at zero gph. Folks who own both a big portable and a small inverter consistently tell me the small one does 70% of the outage hours on 20% of the fuel.

**Kill the phantom heavy loads.** The electric water heater nobody thinks about is 4,500 watts every time it cycles. The space heater in the bathroom is 1,500. During a fuel crunch, flip those breakers off at the panel so they can't ambush your budget — consumption you scheduled is fine, consumption that surprises you at 3 a.m. is how a gas can empties early.

**Keep loads steady when you do run.** Engines are happiest and most efficient per watt in the 50–75% band. Batch your heavy chores into the generator's on-blocks — laundry, pumping, cooking, charging everything — rather than dribbling them out across the day at 10% load, where most of your fuel is just spinning the engine.

## How do you store 5 gallons safely — and refuel without burning the garage down?

Two rules I don't bend on. First, **gasoline has a shelf life.** Untreated pump gas starts degrading in 30–90 days and is genuinely stale by six months. Add stabilizer the day you fill the can — a bottle runs $8–12 and treats 40–80 gallons — and stabilized fuel keeps 12–24 months. Rotate your stored cans into your car every six months and refill with fresh, stabilized gas. The generator that won't start on outage day is almost always drinking last year's fuel; if that's your situation, our [generator won't start troubleshooting guide](/articles/generator-wont-start-troubleshooting/) walks the fix in order.

Second, **never refuel a running or hot generator.** Shut it down and wait at least 5–10 minutes — mufflers run hot enough to ignite gasoline vapor on contact, and a "quick top-off" with the engine running is the single most common generator fire scenario I hear about. Refuel outdoors, on the ground (never in a truck bed, where static can build), and let the machine cool before you restart. Yes, waiting is annoying at midnight in the rain. Do it anyway.

## So — how long will YOUR 5 gallons last?

Work it backwards from your load, not forwards from the gas can. A 2,000W inverter nursing night loads: days. A mid-size portable run sensibly with duty cycling: about a day of real coverage per can, two if you're disciplined. A 7,500W machine running the whole house flat-out: you'll be shaking the can by dinner. The generator doesn't decide how long 5 gallons lasts — your load list does. Write the loads down, do the one division, and you'll know your number before the power company even posts a restoration estimate.
