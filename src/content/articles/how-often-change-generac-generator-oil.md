**Quick answer:** Change a Generac standby generator's oil and filter **first at 25 hours** (the break-in change), then **every 200 hours of runtime or every 2 years, whichever comes first** — that's Generac's published maintenance schedule for air-cooled home standby models. The 2-year limit applies even if the generator barely ran: oil degrades in the sump whether the engine turns or not. At the 25-hour change, you also switch oil types — out goes the factory conventional 5W-30, in goes full synthetic (Generac's 5W-20 GEO or 5W-30 synthetic; the full story is in our [Generac generator oil type guide](/articles/generac-generator-oil-type)).

Two clocks govern this interval — an hour meter and a calendar — and most owners only know about one of them. This guide explains both, the full Generac maintenance schedule the oil change lives inside, and the situations (marathon outages, exercise-only machines) where the printed schedule needs interpretation.

## What is Generac's official oil change schedule?

Here is the manufacturer's maintenance schedule for air-cooled home standby generators, with the oil items in context:

| Service item | First time | Then | Why it exists |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Oil + oil filter change** | **25 hours** (break-in) | **Every 200 hours or 2 years** | Removes break-in metal; then combats wear + moisture |
| Check oil level | — | Monthly, and daily during long runs | Low oil = shutdown or damage |
| Spark plugs: inspect / replace | — | Inspect at 400 hours or 4 years | Ignition reliability |
| Valve clearance check | — | 400 hours or 4 years | Keeps the valve train in spec |
| Air filter replacement | — | 400 hours or 4 years | Airflow and fuel mixture |
| Battery inspection | — | Annually | The #1 cause of no-starts |

The pattern to notice: **everything on this schedule is hours-or-years, whichever first.** Generac designs for two completely different owners with one schedule — the Florida home whose generator logs 150 hours in a single hurricane season, and the northern home whose unit exercises quietly for years and runs one 6-hour outage. The first owner hits the 200-hour limit; the second hits the calendar limit. Neither gets to skip.

This schedule is also a warranty document in disguise. Generac's warranty expects documented maintenance, so keep the receipts, the dates, and the hour-meter readings in a log — a notebook in the enclosure beats a memory. ## Why is there a 25-hour break-in change?

New engines finish machining themselves. During the first hours of operation, piston rings bed into cylinder walls, and the fine metal from that seating process washes into the oil. It is expected, it is harmless *if removed on schedule*, and it is why the first change comes at 25 hours instead of 200.

Generac pairs this with a specific oil instruction: the unit ships filled with **conventional 5W-30**, and Generac's support guidance says **do not switch to synthetic until after the initial 25-hour oil change**. The factory fill is a sacrificial break-in oil. At the 25-hour service you drain it, change the filter, and refill with the long-term oil — Generac's full synthetic 5W-20 GEO by the book, or the accepted 5W-30 synthetic alternative, per our [oil type guide](/articles/generac-generator-oil-type).

25 hours sounds like a lot of runtime for a standby machine — until you realize a single nasty storm weekend can deliver half of it. Many new installations cross 25 hours in their first year; dealers often bundle the break-in service into the install contract, and it's worth asking yours whether yours did.

## Which clock wins: 200 hours or 2 years?

Both — whichever reaches its limit first. The math explains why the calendar side exists:

- **Weekly exercise:** Generac's automatic exercise cycle runs about 5–12 minutes weekly depending on controller settings. That's roughly **4–10 hours a year** — call it a decade to reach 200 hours on exercise alone.
- **Outages:** A typical home might see 10–40 outage-hours in a year. A bad hurricane or ice-storm year can deliver 100+ hours in a week.
- **The sump doesn't care.** Oil sitting in an outdoor enclosure absorbs moisture from condensation cycles, oxidizes, and accumulates acidic compounds — slowly, but on the calendar, not the hour meter. Two-year-old oil with 30 runtime hours on it is still two-year-old oil.

So the exercise-only generator — the most common kind — lives and dies by the **2-year** rule, and the storm-belt generator lives by the **200-hour** rule. When in doubt, the dealer's answer is an annual service visit that changes oil and filter every year regardless; it overshoots Generac's minimum on the calendar side, and it's what I recommend for anyone who doesn't want to track an hour meter. At $200–$450 a year for professional annual maintenance (the range from our [standby generator cost guide](/articles/standby-generator-cost)), it's cheap insurance on a five-figure system.

## What about after a long continuous outage?

The schedule assumes ordinary duty. A generator that just ran **continuously for 3–5 days** under real load has done the hardest work of its life, and I treat that as an interval event on its own:

- **During the run:** check the oil level daily (these engines can consume oil under sustained load), top up with the same oil type that's in the sump, and note the hours.
- **After the run:** if the event consumed 75–100+ hours, change oil and filter afterward even if the tally says 150 of 200 — heat-cycled, load-cycled oil has spent its additive life faster than the raw hours suggest.
- **Log it.** Write the outage dates and hours in the maintenance record. The next owner, dealer, or warranty claim will thank you.

## How do you check the oil between changes?

The interval only protects you if the level stays right between services, and Generac puts level checks on the schedule for a reason — most oil-related shutdowns are level problems, not interval problems. My routine takes two minutes:

1. **Run the generator briefly first** (a manual 5-minute run warms the oil and gives an honest reading), then switch it OFF at the controller and let it sit a few minutes so oil drains back to the sump. Checking immediately after shutdown reads low; checking stone-cold after months of sitting can read misleadingly too.
2. **Pull the dipstick, wipe, reinsert fully, pull again.** The level belongs between the marks — near the top mark is the target on a machine that may run 100 hours unattended.
3. **Read the oil, not just the level.** Darkening over the interval is normal. Milky or frothy oil means moisture contamination — shorten the interval and find out why (an exercise-only machine in a humid climate is the usual story). Gritty oil on the rag means the change is overdue regardless of what the calendar says.
4. **Top up with the same oil that's in it** — the 5W-20 GEO or 5W-30 synthetic from our [oil type guide](/articles/generac-generator-oil-type) — never whatever is open in the garage. Mixing a conventional top-up into a synthetic fill quietly downgrades the whole sump.

Log the check with the date and hour-meter reading, same as a change. A generator that starts *consuming* oil between checks is telling you something early — a level that drops noticeably between monthly checks, on a machine with no visible leak, is worth a dealer's eyes before storm season, not after.

## How much does a Generac oil change cost?

DIY, the parts are modest: roughly 2 quarts of synthetic oil plus a Generac oil filter — typically **$25–$45 in parts** for an air-cooled model, and 30–45 minutes of your time once you've done it once. Dealer service runs **$200–$450** for a full annual visit (oil, filter, plugs check, battery test, inspection), which buys you the documentation trail as much as the labor. Either route beats the alternative line item — a replacement engine — by orders of magnitude; a standby unit itself runs $10,000–$15,000 installed at the popular 22kW size, per our [Generac generator cost guide](/articles/generac-generator-cost).
