**Quick answer:** All three charge your car fully overnight — the Tesla Universal Wall Connector ($550) and the Emporia Pro (about $449) both deliver 48 amps (11.5 kW), and the ChargePoint Home Flex ($599) tops out at 50 amps (12 kW), an edge almost no car can use. The real differences are the plug, the app, and your panel: Tesla's built-in adapter handles both NACS and J1772 and up to six units can power-share, ChargePoint has the best app and scheduling, and Emporia, paired with its Vue monitor, best avoids a panel upgrade. Expect $1,200–$2,500 all-in installed.

If you've narrowed your home charger search down in 2026, the Tesla Wall Connector vs ChargePoint vs Emporia question is probably the exact shortlist sitting in your browser tabs. It's the same three units I install in customers' garages every week, and it's the same three I get asked about at supply houses. I'm Mike Donovan — licensed electrician, 18 years of residential work — and I've mounted, wired, and troubleshot all three of these chargers more times than I can count.

Here's the honest headline before we get into the weeds: **all three will charge your car overnight without breaking a sweat.** The differences that actually matter are the plug, the app, the price, and how smart the unit is about your electrical panel. Let's go through them properly.

## How Fast Do They Charge?

Within a rounding error of each other. At 48 amps on a 60-amp circuit, both the Tesla and the Emporia deliver 11.5 kW, which works out to roughly 40–44 miles of range per hour on most EVs. The ChargePoint's 50-amp ceiling (12 kW) buys you a theoretical edge — about 2 extra miles per hour — that almost no car can use, because most EVs cap their onboard AC charger at 11.5 kW anyway.

That's the thing nobody tells you at the charger aisle: **the charger rarely sets the speed. Your car's onboard charger does.** A Chevy Bolt tops out at 11.5 kW. A Model 3 tops out at 11.5 kW. Plug any of these three units into any of those cars and you get the same number on the dash in the morning: full.

## The Tesla Universal Wall Connector: The Safe Default

The Tesla unit I'd actually sell you is the **Universal Wall Connector ($550)**, not the cheaper standard one. Here's why: it has a J1772 adapter built right into the dock. Push a button, the handle comes out as a Tesla (NACS) plug. Push the other way, it comes out as J1772. One charger, every EV on the market, no adapter dongle rattling around your garage.

That's not a small thing in 2026. Households are mixed now — a Model Y in one bay, a Hyundai in the other, and a kid's used Bolt on the driveway. The Universal handles all of it without thinking.

Other things it does well:

- **Power sharing that actually works.** Up to six Wall Connectors on one circuit, splitting amps automatically as cars plug in and finish. If you have two EVs and one 60-amp circuit's worth of panel space, this is the cleanest solution I install.
- **Clean install.** The wirebox and faceplate design makes for tidy conduit work, and the unit is compact — it doesn't dominate a garage wall.
- **Four-year warranty,** the best of this group.

The honest complaints: if you don't own a Tesla, you manage it through the Tesla app's guest/owner setup, and the energy reporting is thinner than ChargePoint's or Emporia's. Tesla also doesn't play nicely with utility demand-response programs the way ChargePoint does. And the standard (non-Universal) Wall Connector at ~$450 is NACS-only — fine for a Tesla-only house, limiting for everyone else.

## The ChargePoint Home Flex: The App Is the Product

The **ChargePoint Home Flex ($549–$649)** is the charger for people who like data and control. ChargePoint also runs the biggest public charging network in North America, and it shows: the app is the most polished of the three, with clean scheduling, accurate energy cost tracking, Alexa integration, and reminders if you forget to plug in.

The hardware is genuinely flexible — hence "Flex." An electrician can set it anywhere from 16 to 50 amps. Hardwired on a 70-amp circuit it delivers the full 50 amps (12 kW). Plugged into a NEMA 14-50 or 6-50 outlet, it's capped at 40 amps, which is still 30-plus miles per hour. That adjustability makes it the easiest charger to fit into a crowded panel.

Now the gripe, and it's a real one: **the Home Flex requires WiFi for setup.** No WiFi, no activation — the smart features are the product, and ChargePoint won't let you run it as a dumb charger out of the box. If your garage has weak WiFi (many do; it's usually the farthest room from the router), plan on a range extender or hardwire access point. I've been on installs where we spent longer fighting garage WiFi than mounting the unit.

Also know that at $599 street price, you're paying a premium for software. If you never open charging apps — most people stop after month two — that premium buys you nothing over the Emporia.

## The Emporia Pro: Best Value, Secret Weapon

The **Emporia Pro ($399–$549, usually ~$449)** is the one I recommend when a customer cares about money and energy, in that order. Charging-wise it's a peer of the Tesla: 48 amps, 11.5 kW, 24-foot cable, solid build. The app is less beautiful than ChargePoint's but shows you *more* — real-time watts, cost per session, solar export if you have panels.

The secret weapon is the ecosystem. Pair the Pro with the **Emporia Vue home energy monitor (~$150–$200)** clipped into your panel, and the charger can watch your whole house's load and throttle itself down when the oven, dryer, and AC all run at once — then speed back up when the house calms down. In plain terms: **it can keep you from needing a $1,500–$3,000 panel upgrade** on a maxed-out 100-amp service. Neither Tesla nor ChargePoint offers anything this effective at this price. I've installed this combo in three 1970s houses this year where the alternative quote started with "new panel."

The trade-offs are fit and finish. The unit looks like what it is — a value-engineered plastic box — and the app's interface assumes you enjoy numbers. If you do enjoy numbers, you'll love it. Emporia is also a smaller company than Tesla or ChargePoint; the 3-year warranty is standard, and their support has been good in my experience, but it's a fair point to weigh.

## Installation Notes From the Truck

The chargers differ more on my ladder than they do on your phone. The Tesla's two-piece design — wirebox first, unit snaps on — is the fastest clean install of the three: I land the conductors, level the box, and click the face on in minutes, and the terminals are laid out like someone actually thought about the person holding the screwdriver. ChargePoint's real install gift is flexibility. If you already have a NEMA 14-50 outlet in the garage, the Home Flex can be running in twenty minutes. But hardwiring it for the full 50 amps means a 70-amp breaker and heavier wire, which surprises people who budgeted it as a plug-in job. The Emporia is the most conventional install of the three: standard knockouts, standard terminals, nothing clever — which also means nothing exotic to break. It's the one I'd call genuinely electrician-proof.

One warning that applies to all three, and I put it in every quote: EV charging is a **continuous load** under the electrical code. A 48-amp charger needs a 60-amp circuit, full stop. If a handyman quotes you a 50-amp breaker for a 48-amp charger "because it'll be fine," it will not be fine at 2 a.m. in July when the car, the AC, and the dryer are all running. Pull the permit. The inspection is the cheapest insurance on the entire job, and it's the reason your homeowner's policy pays out if anything ever goes wrong.

## Honorable Mention: The Grizzl-E

Not everyone wants a smart charger. If your attitude is "just charge the car," look at the **Grizzl-E (~$399–$470)** from United Chargers. It's a Canadian-built aluminum brick of a charger: 40 or 48 amps depending on model, no WiFi required, dip switches inside to set the amperage, and a reputation for surviving winters that kill lesser units. There's no app to speak of — your car's app handles scheduling. United Chargers backs it with a 3-year warranty standard (5 years available on some models) and support staffed by people who actually know what a dip switch is. If it dies, it dies simple — no bricked smart features, no account lockout, just a charger that stopped charging, which is exactly how a garage appliance should fail. For a barn, a workshop, or anyone who's sick of making accounts to use appliances, it's the most reliable dumb charger I know. It loses this comparison on features, full stop — but it deserves a seat at the table for people who don't want the features.

## Which EV Charger Should You Buy?

- **You own a Tesla (or one Tesla and anything else):** Tesla Universal Wall Connector. The dual-plug dock and power sharing make it the obvious install, and the 4-year warranty seals it.
- **You love apps, schedules, and utility programs:** ChargePoint Home Flex. Best software in the business, genuinely useful energy tracking, and the adjustable amperage fits tough panels — as long as your garage WiFi reaches it.
- **You're watching the budget or your panel is nearly full:** Emporia Pro, ideally with the Vue monitor. Same charging speed as the Tesla for $100 less, and the load management can save you thousands on electrical work.
- **You hate all of the above:** Grizzl-E. Plug it in, charge the car, think about something else.
