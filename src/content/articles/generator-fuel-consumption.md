The number-one surprise first-time generator owners report isn't the noise, the weight, or the maintenance. It's how fast the thing drinks fuel. A mid-size portable generator running half the day will burn through more gasoline in 48 hours than a pickup truck does in a month of commuting. If you don't plan for it, you'll be standing in a gas-station line at hour 30 of an outage, watching the price board and doing math you should have done in the store aisle.

Here's the fuel consumption picture straight — gallons per hour by generator size and load, how each fuel compares, how to calculate your actual runtime, and what a multi-day outage really costs.

## The core rule: consumption follows load, not size

A generator burns fuel in proportion to how much power it's actually producing, not its maximum rating. A 7,500-watt unit idling along at 2,000 watts uses roughly the same fuel as a 3,500-watt unit carrying the same 2,000-watt load. The bigger machine just has more headroom — and slightly worse efficiency at very light loads, since it's still dragging a bigger engine around.

This is why the "50% load" number is the one manufacturers publish and the one you should care about. Most homes on backup power run a generator somewhere between 40–60% of its rating. Run everything you own at once and you'll approach the full-load number; run just the fridge and lights and you'll be well under the quarter-load figure.

What follows are typical consumption ranges for gasoline portables. Every brand varies, but these numbers hold across the mainstream models.

## Gasoline consumption by generator size

| Generator size | At 25% load | At 50% load | At 75% load | At 100% load |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2,000W inverter | 0.12–0.18 gal/hr | 0.18–0.25 gal/hr | 0.25–0.35 gal/hr | 0.35–0.45 gal/hr |
| 3,500W portable | 0.2–0.3 gal/hr | 0.3–0.45 gal/hr | 0.45–0.6 gal/hr | 0.6–0.8 gal/hr |
| 5,000W portable | 0.35–0.5 gal/hr | 0.5–0.75 gal/hr | 0.7–1.0 gal/hr | 1.0–1.3 gal/hr |
| 7,500W portable | 0.45–0.65 gal/hr | 0.7–1.0 gal/hr | 1.0–1.4 gal/hr | 1.4–1.8 gal/hr |
| 10,000W portable | 0.6–0.85 gal/hr | 1.0–1.4 gal/hr | 1.4–1.9 gal/hr | 1.9–2.4 gal/hr |
| 12,000W portable | 0.75–1.0 gal/hr | 1.2–1.6 gal/hr | 1.7–2.2 gal/hr | 2.2–2.8 gal/hr |
| 20kW standby (NG) | — | — | — | see gas section below |

Inverter generators deserve a callout: because they throttle the engine speed down at light loads, their quarter-load and half-load numbers are dramatically better than a conventional portable's. A 2,000W inverter sipping 0.15 gal/hr at a light load is a real number. A conventional 3,500W unit at the same actual wattage won't match it — the engine runs full speed regardless, so it burns closer to its 50%-load figure.

For standby units (the ones that run on natural gas or propane permanently installed outside), fuel is usually measured in cubic feet per hour rather than gallons. I'll cover those numbers in the fuel comparison below.

## Propane vs gasoline vs natural gas consumption

Same generator, different fuel, different consumption — and the differences aren't small. Here's how a typical 7,500W portable behaves at 50% load on each fuel:

| Fuel | Consumption at 50% load | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Gasoline | 0.7–1.0 gal/hr | Full rated wattage available |
| Propane | 1.0–1.4 gal/hr (equivalent) | Roughly 10% less wattage output; propane has ~27% less energy per gallon than gasoline |
| Natural gas | ~1.5–2.0 therms/hr (typical standby) | Unlimited supply from the pipe; standby units only |

Propane's lower energy density (about 91,500 BTU per gallon vs ~114,000 for gasoline) means the generator burns more volume to make the same power. It also means roughly 10% less peak wattage — a 9,500W-rated dual-fuel unit delivers around 8,500–8,600W on propane. This isn't a flaw; it's physics. Size accordingly.

Natural gas is measured differently — about 1,037 BTU per cubic foot, and utilities bill in therms (100,000 BTU each). A 20kW standby generator at half load pulls roughly 200–250 cubic feet per hour. At full load, 300+ cubic feet per hour. Your meter needs to be sized for it — many residential meters are, but check with your gas utility before installing a big standby unit.

## Runtime math: what common tanks actually buy you

Manufacturers quote "runtime at 50% load" because it's the standard. But what you care about is simpler: how many hours until I have to refuel? Here's the math for common setups.

**Formula:** Runtime (hours) = Tank capacity (gallons) ÷ consumption (gal/hr at your load)

### Gasoline portable tanks

| Generator | Tank size | Runtime at 50% load | Runtime at 25% load |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2,000W inverter | ~1.0 gal | 4–5.5 hrs | 6–8 hrs |
| 3,500W portable | ~4 gal | 9–13 hrs | 13–20 hrs |
| 5,000W portable | ~5 gal | 7–10 hrs | 10–14 hrs |
| 7,500W portable | ~6–7 gal | 6–10 hrs | 9–15 hrs |
| 10,000W portable | ~8–9 gal | 6–9 hrs | 9–14 hrs |

A 7,500W portable with a 6-gallon tank at half load: 6 ÷ 0.85 ≈ 7 hours. That means two refuels per day if you're running around the clock. Plan your fuel stock around that reality, not the brochure's best-case number.

### Propane tank runtimes (7,500W generator at 50% load, ~1.1–1.3 gal/hr propane)

| Tank | Capacity | Runtime at 50% load |
|---|---|---|
| 20-lb grill tank | ~4.6–4.7 gal | 3.5–4.5 hrs |
| 30-lb tank | ~7.0 gal | 5–6.5 hrs |
| 100-lb cylinder | ~23.6 gal | 18–22 hrs |
| 100-gallon stationary tank | ~80 gal (filled to 80%) | 60–75 hrs |
| 250-gallon tank | ~200 gal (filled to 80%) | 150–185 hrs |

That 20-lb grill tank number catches people off guard: less than 5 hours on a mid-size generator at half load. If propane is your fuel, either keep multiple cylinders on hand or step up to a 100-lb cylinder or larger. A 100-lb cylinder is the sweet spot for most homeowners — big enough to matter, small enough to have delivered or exchanged without special equipment.

### Standby on natural gas: effectively unlimited

As long as the gas line holds pressure, a natural-gas standby runs indefinitely. During hurricanes and ice storms, residential gas service is rarely interrupted — it's one of the strongest arguments for a standby unit. For propane-fed standbys, runtime is whatever your tank holds; a 500-gallon underground tank (filled to 80% = ~400 gallons) running a 20kW unit at half load (~2.5–3 gal/hr propane) buys you roughly 130–160 hours, or about a week of continuous operation.

## Cost per day of outage

This is the number nobody runs until they're living it. At roughly $3.20–$3.60 per gallon of gasoline (a reasonable 2026 planning figure; your state varies):

| Generator | Daily runtime | Fuel per day | Cost per day |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3,500W portable @ 50% | 12 hrs | ~4–5 gal | $13–$18 |
| 5,000W portable @ 50% | 16 hrs | ~9–12 gal | $29–$43 |
| 7,500W portable @ 50% | 20 hrs | ~14–20 gal | $45–$72 |
| 10,000W portable @ 50% | 24 hrs | ~24–34 gal | $77–$122 |
| 20kW standby (NG) @ 50% | 24 hrs | ~55–60 therms* | $60–$120** |

\* 20kW standby at 50% ≈ 2.2–2.5 therms/hr × 24
\*\* Natural gas residential rates vary wildly by state ($1–$2 per therm typical); check your bill.

A 5-day outage on a 7,500W portable at $55/day is $275 in fuel alone — more than many people paid for a quarter of the generator's purchase price. A 10-day outage approaches $550. This is the honest math that belongs in every backup-power plan: the generator is the cheap part; feeding it is the expensive part. ## Fuel storage limits and stabilizer

### Gasoline

Untreated gasoline starts breaking down in about 30 days. By 3–6 months it's unreliable, and the varnish residue it leaves is the number-one cause of dead generators at outage time. The rules:

- **Add stabilizer (Sta-Bil or equivalent) to every gallon you store.** It extends storage life to 12–24 months.
- **Rotate your stock.** Use stored gas in your car or mower and refill — the "fill once and forget" method fails even with stabilizer if you stretch it past two years.
- **Store in approved containers, 5 gallons or smaller per container**, in a cool, ventilated spot away from the house — a detached shed or garage, never a basement or near a water heater or furnace.
- **Most fire codes cap residential gasoline storage at 25 gallons** total (check your local code — it varies by municipality).
- Fill generators with stabilized gas and run the carburetor dry before storage if the unit sits for months.

### Propane

Propane doesn't degrade, ever. A sealed tank sits for a decade and the fuel is identical. That's its single biggest advantage as a standby fuel. Store tanks upright, outdoors, at least 10 feet from the house and any ignition source. Keep valves closed and caps on when not connected.

### What you cannot do

Never store fuel — gasoline or propane — inside living spaces, attached garages (check local code; many jurisdictions restrict this), or near heat sources. Never siphon by mouth. Never smoke anywhere near refueling. And never refuel a running or hot generator: shut it off, let the engine cool for at least 15 minutes, then refuel outdoors with the cap replaced and any spills wiped before restarting. Hot exhaust on a generator that just ran under load can hit 500°F+ — gasoline vapor ignites at far lower temperatures than people expect.

## Reducing consumption: the load-management trick

You can't change how much fuel a generator burns per watt-hour, but you can change how many watt-hours you need. The single biggest lever is cycling: run the generator for the heavy loads (fridge cooldown, well pump, cooking), then shut it down. A fridge only needs 1–2 hours of cooling every 4–6 hours to hold safe temperatures. The well pump runs a few minutes per cycle. Running 24/7 to power a few lights between the heavy loads is pure waste.

A practical approach for a multi-day outage:

1. Run the generator 2–3 hours in the morning (fridge, freezer, phones, coffee, water heater if needed)
2. Shut down midday — unplug everything
3. Run 3–4 hours in the evening (dinner, showers, recharge devices)
4. Optionally one overnight hour if the fridge needs it
