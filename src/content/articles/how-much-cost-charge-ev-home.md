Home EV charging cost is one of those questions with a simple answer and a complicated truth. The simple answer: at the 2026 US average residential rate of about **$0.16 per kWh**, driving 1,000 miles a month costs roughly **$40 to $53 in electricity** — about a third of what you'd spend on gas. The complicated truth: your state, your rate plan, your car's efficiency, and when you charge can swing that number from under $30 to over $120 for the exact same miles.

I'm an electrician, not an economist, but I've done this math on napkins for enough customers that I can walk you through it in five minutes. No hype, no "save $10,000 a year" nonsense — just the arithmetic.

## How Much Does It Cost to Charge an EV at Home? The Short Answer

Three numbers decide everything:

1. **Your electricity rate** ($/kWh) — the price on your utility bill.
2. **Your EV's efficiency** (miles per kWh) — how far the car goes on each unit of electricity.
3. **Your miles** — how much you drive.

The formula is dead simple:

> **Monthly cost = (miles driven ÷ miles per kWh) × your rate per kWh**

Example: 1,000 miles ÷ 3.5 mi/kWh = 286 kWh × $0.16 = **$45.76/month**.

That's it. That's the whole calculation. Everything below is just filling in realistic values for those three inputs — and showing you where the gotchas hide.

## The kWh Math: EV Efficiency and Cost Per Mile

Most modern EVs land between **3 and 4 miles per kWh** in real-world mixed driving. (The EPA's MPGe number confuses everyone — ignore it for this math and use mi/kWh, which some cars display directly.)

Real-world efficiency by vehicle type:

| Vehicle type | Typical efficiency | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Efficient sedan | 3.8–4.2 mi/kWh | Tesla Model 3, Hyundai Ioniq 6, Chevy Equinox EV |
| Average crossover | 3.0–3.5 mi/kWh | Tesla Model Y, Ford Mustang Mach-E, VW ID.4 |
| Large SUV / truck | 2.2–2.8 mi/kWh | Ford F-150 Lightning, Rivian R1S, GMC Hummer EV (~1.5–1.8, it's a brick) |
| Winter driving (any EV) | Subtract 20–30% | Cold batteries are thirsty batteries |

Cost per mile at the $0.16/kWh national average:

- 4.0 mi/kWh → **$0.040/mile**
- 3.5 mi/kWh → **$0.046/mile**
- 3.0 mi/kWh → **$0.053/mile**
- 2.5 mi/kWh → **$0.064/mile**

Compare that to gas: a 30-mpg car at $3.30/gallon costs **$0.11/mile**. Even the thirstiest electric truck at average electricity rates costs roughly half per mile of an efficient gas car. That per-mile gap is the entire economic case for EVs, and it's real — just make sure you're comparing against YOUR electricity rate, not the national average.

Here's what nobody tells you about efficiency numbers: the window sticker lies a little, and winter lies a lot. That 3.5 mi/kWh crossover might do 3.9 in May and 2.6 in January in Minnesota. If you're doing this math to decide whether an EV pencils out, use the winter number for your climate. Being pleasantly surprised beats being short.

## Home EV Charging Cost by State: What You Pay Per kWh (2026)

This is where the national average becomes actively misleading. Residential electricity in the US ranges from about ten cents to over forty-five cents per kWh — a 4x spread. Your charging cost follows it exactly.

**Cheapest states (~$0.10–$0.13/kWh):** Louisiana, Idaho, Washington, Utah, Mississippi, North Dakota, Nebraska. Hydro and cheap natural gas do the heavy lifting here. At $0.11/kWh and 3.5 mi/kWh, your cost per mile is **$0.031** — about three cents a mile. That's almost absurdly cheap driving.

**Middle states (~$0.14–$0.19/kWh):** Texas, Florida, Ohio, Pennsylvania, Illinois, Georgia, Arizona, Nevada, the Carolinas. Most of the country lives here. At $0.16/kWh, 1,000 miles costs that familiar **$40–$53**.

**Expensive states (~$0.22–$0.32/kWh):** California, New York, New Jersey, Massachusetts, Connecticut, Rhode Island, New Hampshire, Vermont. At $0.30/kWh, 1,000 miles at 3.5 mi/kWh costs **$86** — still cheaper than gas, but the savings story gets thinner.

**Hawaii (~$0.40–$0.45/kWh):** the outlier. At $0.42/kWh, those same 1,000 miles cost **$120** — roughly at parity with gas, sometimes worse. Hawaii is the one state where the fuel-savings pitch needs an asterisk the size of the island.

Quick reference table — monthly cost for 1,000 miles at 3.5 mi/kWh:

| Rate tier | $/kWh | Monthly cost (1,000 mi) | vs. gas ($110/mo) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cheap states | $0.11 | ~$31 | Save ~$79 |
| US average | $0.16 | ~$46 | Save ~$64 |
| Expensive states | $0.28 | ~$80 | Save ~$30 |
| Hawaii | $0.42 | ~$120 | ~Break-even |

Two honest notes. First, your bill's "rate" isn't always the number to use — check whether your utility quotes the all-in rate (generation + delivery + fees) or just generation. The all-in number is what the EV actually costs you. Second, some utilities have increasing-block rates where your marginal kWh — the ones the EV adds on top — are billed at a higher tier. If your household already sits in tier 2, the EV's electricity costs the tier-2 rate, not your average. Read the bill, not the brochure.

## Time-of-Use Rates: Charging Overnight Cuts Your Bill in Half

Here's the move that changes the math more than anything else: **time-of-use (TOU) billing.** Many utilities charge dramatically different rates by hour — expensive on weekday afternoons and evenings (4–9pm is the classic peak window), cheap overnight.

Typical 2026 TOU spreads:

- **Overnight/off-peak:** $0.08–$0.14/kWh
- **Daytime peak:** $0.25–$0.45/kWh

Real examples: California's PG&E EV2-A plan has overnight rates around $0.30/kWh off-peak versus $0.60+/kWh peak in summer (California is its own special pain — even the "cheap" rate is expensive). In friendlier territories like parts of Texas, Georgia, and the Midwest, overnight EV rates of **$0.08–$0.10/kWh** are common. At $0.09/kWh, 1,000 miles costs **$26/month**. That's less than one tank of gas.

The beautiful part: EVs are the perfect TOU load. The car sits in the garage all night anyway. You set the charge timer for midnight (every EV and every decent charger has scheduling built in), and you wake up to a full battery bought at the cheapest rate your utility sells. No lifestyle change whatsoever.

The catch — and there's always a catch: switching your whole house to a TOU plan means your daytime electricity gets more expensive too. If someone's home running the AC all afternoon in July, the peak rate can eat your overnight EV savings. Do the math on your total bill, not just the car. Some utilities offer EV-specific sub-metered rates that only apply to the charger circuit — those are the best deal when available, because the house stays on the flat rate. Ask your utility specifically about EV rate plans before you switch anything.

This is also where a home battery earns its keep in a completely different way — charging cheap overnight and covering your expensive evening peak. The [home battery vs generator breakdown](/articles/home-battery-vs-generator/) walks through that arbitrage math if your utility's rate spread is wide enough to matter.

## How Much Does It Cost to Charge an EV for 1,000 Miles a Month?

Let's put it all together with a full worked example — the kind I'd scribble for a customer deciding between keeping the Camry and buying the Model Y.

**Assumptions:** 1,000 miles/month, EV at 3.3 mi/kWh (crossover, real world), $0.16/kWh flat rate. Gas comparison: 30-mpg car, $3.30/gallon.

**EV monthly "fuel" cost:**
1,000 ÷ 3.3 = 303 kWh × $0.16 = **$48.50**

**Gas monthly fuel cost:**
1,000 ÷ 30 = 33.3 gallons × $3.30 = **$110**

**Monthly savings: ~$61.50. Annual: ~$738.**

Now run it with overnight TOU at $0.10/kWh: 303 × $0.10 = **$30.30/month**, saving ~$80/month or **~$955/year** versus gas.

And the flip side — California at $0.30/kWh flat: 303 × $0.30 = **$91/month**, saving only ~$19/month. Still positive, but the payback on a $1,500 charger install stretches from "a couple years" to "most of a decade" on fuel savings alone.

Scale it to your driving: at 15,000 miles a year (a common lease allowance), the average-rate savings versus a 30-mpg gas car run about **$920/year**. Heavy drivers — 20,000+ miles a year, rideshare, long commutes — save the most in absolute dollars, which is exactly why high-mileage drivers are the best EV candidates economically.

What about the charger install cost payback? A $1,500 Level 2 install (see the [installation cost breakdown](/articles/level-2-ev-charger-installation-cost/)) paid back purely from fuel savings takes roughly two years at average rates and average mileage. But that's the wrong framing — you'd pay for "fueling infrastructure" with a gas car too, in the form of driving to gas stations. The honest payback includes your time, which nobody prices.

## The Hidden Costs: Charging Losses, Vampire Drain, and Demand Charges

The math above is slightly optimistic. Here's what shaves the edges off:

**Charging losses (~8–12%).** Not every kWh from the wall makes it into the battery. The onboard charger, battery heating/cooling during charging, and AC-DC conversion eat roughly a tenth. If the car reports it accepted 270 kWh this month, your meter spun about 300 kWh. Add 10% to every number above for honesty. That $48.50 becomes ~$53.

**Vampire drain (1–3 kWh/day).** EVs sip power while parked — battery thermal management, sentry modes, always-on computers. A Tesla with Sentry Mode on can burn 200–300W continuously, which is 5–7 kWh a day just sitting there — potentially $25+/month at average rates for a feature that's essentially a dashcam. Turn Sentry Mode off at home (geofenced) and the drain drops to a trickle. This one surprises people more than anything else on this list.

**Level 1 vs Level 2 efficiency.** Charging on a standard 120V outlet (Level 1) is measurably less efficient than 240V Level 2 — the car's computers run for 20+ hours instead of 6, and fixed overhead gets amortized over fewer kWh per hour. The difference is roughly 5–8% more wall energy per battery kWh on Level 1. Not a dealbreaker, but another quiet tax on the slowest charging.

**Demand charges (rare for residential, check anyway).** A few utilities with residential demand billing can ding you for the peak kW draw of a 48A charger (11.5 kW) even if total kWh is modest. If your utility has demand charges, schedule charging overnight AND consider a lower-amperage setting. Most residential customers never see demand charges, but the ones who do get surprised.

**The panel and install cost.** Already covered in detail in the [Level 2 installation cost guide](/articles/level-2-ev-charger-installation-cost/) — $800 to $4,000+ depending on your situation. It's a one-time cost, but it's real money that belongs in the payback math.

None of these flip the economics by themselves. Together they trim maybe 15–20% off the naive savings number. Still solidly cheaper than gas in 49 states.

## When Home Charging Savings Don't Pencil Out

You asked for opinions, so here's mine: the "EVs are always cheaper to run" line is marketing, and there are real situations where it doesn't hold.

**You barely drive.** At 400 miles a month, the fuel savings are ~$25/month at average rates. A $1,500 charger install takes five years to pay back on fuel alone. The EV still might make sense for other reasons, but don't buy it for the fuel math at low mileage.

**You're in Hawaii or on a punishing California peak rate** and you can't or won't use TOU scheduling. At true parity with gas, the economic case evaporates and you're buying the EV for other reasons — which is fine, just be honest about it.

**You can't charge at home.** This is the big one. If you're in an apartment or condo with no dedicated parking and no charger, you're living on public charging at $0.35–$0.60/kWh. At those rates the per-mile cost meets or beats gas, the convenience is worse, and the whole proposition crumbles. Public-charging-dependent EV ownership is the most expensive way to drive electric. I tell renters this straight: sort the charging situation before the car.

**Your utility's fixed fees are high.** Some utilities have $30–$50/month fixed customer charges regardless of usage. Those don't change with an EV, but they shrink the *percentage* savings people expect when they compare total bills.

**You'd need a $4,000 service upgrade for the charger.** The fuel savings don't cover it for a decade. In that situation, I'd honestly suggest: try Level 1 charging on your existing garage outlet for a month first. At 3–5 miles of range per hour, an overnight Level 1 session adds 30–50 miles — which covers a huge number of actual commutes. Plenty of people discover they never needed Level 2 at all, and the install cost drops to zero. Start cheap, upgrade only if the math demands it.

The through-line: home charging is cheap *when you have cheap overnight electricity and a place to plug in*. Those two conditions do 90% of the work. Everything else is optimization.

## The Bottom Line on Home EV Charging Cost

At average US rates, home EV charging costs roughly **4–6 cents a mile** — about a third of gas. Time-of-use overnight rates can push that under 3 cents. Your state determines whether you save $80 a month or $20, and vampire drain plus charging losses trim about 10–15% off the naive math. The economics are genuinely good for most homeowners with a garage and a decent utility rate — and genuinely bad for apartment dwellers stuck on public chargers.
