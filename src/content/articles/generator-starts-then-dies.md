Your generator starts then dies — almost more frustrating than one that won't start at all. It catches, it revs, you think you're back in business — and three seconds later it's silent again. I've been a licensed electrician for 18 years, and I get this exact call after nearly every storm: "Mike, it starts, so it can't be serious, right?" Usually it isn't. But the fix depends entirely on *when* it dies, so watch the timing before you touch a wrench.

Good news: a generator that starts has proven it has spark, compression, and at least some fuel — half the problems in my [generator won't start troubleshooting guide](/articles/generator-wont-start-troubleshooting) are already ruled out. What's left is a short list of causes that let an engine fire but won't let it keep running. We'll work them in order, most likely first.

Before you touch anything, three safety rules:

1. **Disconnect the spark plug wire** before any inspection or repair beyond starting checks. You do not want the engine firing while your fingers are near moving parts.
2. **Work on a cool engine.** Generators get extremely hot. If you've been restarting it over and over, give it 10–15 minutes to cool down.
3. **Handle fuel carefully.** Work outdoors or in a ventilated garage. No smoking, no open flames, wipe up spills immediately.

## How long does it run before it dies — and why does that matter?

Time it — how long it runs before quitting is your best clue, because each cause has its own signature:

| Run time before dying | Most likely cause |
|---|---|
| 2–10 seconds, every time | Low-oil shutdown sensor |
| 30 seconds to 2 minutes | Fuel starvation — filter, line, or tank vent |
| Runs on choke, dies off choke | Partially clogged carburetor main jet |
| Dies as it warms up (5–15 min) | Choke left on, fouled plug, clogged air filter |
| Dies the instant a load hits | Usually *not* this problem — see the overload section below |

## Is the low-oil sensor shutting it down?

This is the #1 cause I find on portables, and the one people resist believing. "But it has oil in it," they tell me. Then we put the unit on flat concrete, pull the dipstick — a quarter inch low.

Here's what's happening: most modern generators have a **low-oil shutdown sensor** that kills the spark when the oil drops below a safe level. At startup the oil sits settled and the sensor is happy. The engine fires, oil starts circulating, the level at the sensor dips — and it dies within **2–10 seconds**. It'll restart happily and do the exact same thing, all day long.

**Symptoms that point to it:** dies at almost exactly the same point, a few seconds after starting, every time — and it may run slightly longer tipped one way on a slope than the other.

**The check:** Put the generator on truly level ground — not your lawn, not a sloped driveway. Pull the dipstick or fill cap; on most portables the oil should be at or near the **top of the fill neck**. Check twice: wipe, reinsert, read again.

**The fix:** Top off with the oil weight in your manual, typically **SAE 10W-30**, for about **$5–7** a quart (most small engines take 0.6–1.1 quarts total). Don't overfill — oil filled to the threads foams and can trigger the same sensor. This is why oil checks are step one of the [generator maintenance checklist](/articles/generator-maintenance-checklist) I give every customer: five seconds with a dipstick beats an hour with a carburetor.

One warning: if the oil is full and it still dies in seconds, don't just unplug the sensor and run it. Sensors do fail (**$10–25** to replace), but a sensor telling the truth is the only thing between you and a seized engine.

## Is the engine starving for fuel?

If it runs 30 seconds to a couple of minutes and then fades out — revs dropping, a little surging, then silence — the engine is drinking the carburetor bowl dry faster than fuel can refill it. (That's different from my [generator keeps shutting off](/articles/generator-keeps-shutting-off) guide, which covers engines that quit after running fine for a while.) Here the supply is restricted from the start. Three usual suspects:

### Could the fuel filter be clogged?

Inline filters are small, cheap, and forgotten for years. Sediment, rust flakes, and varnish slowly pack them until flow drops to a trickle — enough to fill the bowl while the engine sits, nowhere near enough to feed it running.

**The check:** Find the filter (a small clear or white cylinder in the fuel line between tank and carburetor). Debris or dark discoloration inside means it's done. Better test: pull the fuel line off the carburetor inlet, aim it into a gas can, and open the valve. You want a strong, steady stream; a lazy dribble means the restriction is upstream — filter, valve, or tank outlet screen.

**The fix:** Replace the filter. Match the size (most are 1/4-inch line) and mind the flow-direction arrow. **$5–12**, five minutes.

### Is the fuel line pinched or kinked?

Fuel lines get pinched when a tank is reinstalled after service, crushed by a storage strap, or collapse internally as the rubber ages in ethanol fuel. From outside, a line can look fine while the inside has swollen nearly shut.

**The check:** Follow the line from tank to carburetor with your fingers, feeling for flat spots, sharp bends, or a section that feels mushy. The flow test helps too: good flow at the tank outlet but poor flow at the carburetor end means the line itself is the problem.

**The fix:** Replace it with fuel-rated hose, about **$1–2 per foot**, routed in gentle curves. Random hardware-store tubing that isn't rated for gasoline will swell, and you'll be doing this again.

### Is the tank vent blocked? Try the gas-cap test

This is my favorite diagnostic in small-engine repair — free, and it takes thirty seconds. Generator tanks vent through the gas cap: as fuel flows out, air has to flow in. If that vent clogs (dust, debris, a mud-dauber nest), a vacuum builds in the tank, fuel flow stops, and the engine dies. Wait a few minutes, air seeps in, and it starts right up again. Owners describe it as "it dies, but if I let it sit, it runs again," and they assume it's haunted.

**The test:** Start it with the cap loose — cracked open, not removed, so fuel can't splash out. If it now runs indefinitely, the vent in the cap is blocked.

**The fix:** Some caps can be cleaned (pick out the vent passage and blow it clear); most people just replace the cap for **$10–20**. Worth it — a vacuum-starved engine runs lean and hot while it struggles, and it throws your [fuel consumption and runtime numbers](/articles/generator-fuel-consumption) off while it lasts.

## Does it run on choke but die when you open the choke?

If the engine runs — roughly — with the choke on, and dies the moment you move it to RUN, stop guessing. That's a **partially clogged carburetor main jet**, almost always from stale fuel.

Here's the logic: the choke enriches the mixture by restricting air. If the main jet is half-blocked with varnish, the engine is starved at normal mixture settings, and the choke's extra richness compensates just enough to keep it alive. Open the choke and it goes lean and dies. I see this constantly on generators that sat with untreated gas — gasoline degrades in as little as 30 days, and ethanol blends leave gum behind as they evaporate out of the bowl. The other telltales: months-old, sour-smelling fuel, and surging — if surging is the main event, see my [generator surging guide](/articles/generator-surging) too, since a partially clogged jet causes both. Honda models are especially prone to presenting exactly this way, which is why we cover the Honda-specific version in our guide on [why a Honda generator only runs with the choke on](/articles/honda-generator-only-runs-with-choke).

**The check:** Drain a little fuel from the carburetor bowl into a clear jar. Fresh gas is pale and clean-smelling. Yellow, dark, or varnish-smelling fuel — or any grit or jelly-like blobs — confirms the fuel system needs attention, not just the jet.

**The fix:** Clean the carburetor (pull the float bowl and main jet, soak in carb cleaner at **$10–15** a can, clear the jet with soft copper wire — never steel, which enlarges it) or replace it: aftermarket carburetors for common portables run **$15–35**, and a rebuild kit **$10–25**. Either way, drain the old fuel and refill with fresh gas plus stabilizer (**$8–12** a bottle), or you'll clog the new jet with the same bad fuel by next month.

## Did you leave the choke on?

Embarrassing, common, and free to fix — so check it before you buy parts. If the choke stays closed as the engine warms up, the mixture gets richer and richer until the plug fouls and the engine drowns in its own fuel. It typically starts perfectly, runs **5–15 minutes**, then blubbers, blows a little black smoke, and dies. Restarts produce a flooded engine: strong fuel smell, wet plug.

**The check:** Look at the choke lever. Actually look at it — on combination choke/throttle levers it's easy to leave it one notch short of full RUN. On auto-choke models, watch the choke plate while someone cranks: it should open as the engine warms. A stuck linkage or failed solenoid (**$15–40**) holds it shut.

**The fix:** Move the choke fully to RUN once the engine has run 30–60 seconds. If it's already flooded, choke OFF, wait five minutes (or pull and dry the plug), and try again. Free a stuck auto-choke linkage with carb cleaner and a drop of oil; replace the solenoid if it won't move with power applied.

## Is the spark plug fouling out?

A plug that's fouled or worn can fire well enough to start a cold engine, then break down as it heats up and cylinder pressures rise. The engine warms for a few minutes, starts misfiring, and dies — and often restarts once it cools, which confuses everyone.

**The check:** Pull the plug — five minutes with a plug socket. Spec gap on most portables is **0.028–0.031 inches**. Read what you find: black and sooty means rich running or chronic choke use; oily means oil getting past rings or valve seals; a worn, rounded center electrode with a gap drifted well past spec means it's simply used up. A plug tells the truth about what the engine has been burning.

**The fix:** Replace it. A plug is **$3–8**; cleaning a fouled one is false economy. If the *new* plug fouls within a few hours, the plug was a symptom, not the cause — go back to the choke and carburetor sections. Keep a spare in the toolkit; plug life is part of the bigger picture in [how long generators last](/articles/how-long-do-generators-last).

## Is the air filter choking it off?

An engine is an air pump. A filter clogged with dust, saturated foam, or — my personal record — a mouse nest built over one winter lets an engine start on the air in the intake, then strangles it: muffled, rich, losing revs, dark smoke, dead.

**The check:** Pull the filter cover and hold the element up to the light. Paper you can't see light through is done; foam that's crumbling, oil-soaked, or caked is done. Check the intake tube for nesting material while you're there.

**The fix:** Paper filters get replaced (**$8–20**), never "cleaned" with an air hose, which tears the media. Foam can be washed, dried fully, and re-oiled, squeezed tacky — not dripping.

## Could it just be overload? Probably not — here's the difference

People often blame overload, so let's be precise. A startup-surge problem looks like this: the engine runs fine, a motor load kicks on (well pump, AC compressor, fridge), the engine **bogs and labors**, and either it recovers, the breaker trips, or it stalls *under that load*. It dies **when the load hits** — not seconds after starting with nothing connected.

If yours dies with nothing plugged in, it is not overload. Full stop. Work the list above. If it only dies when a specific appliance starts, compare that appliance's starting watts against the generator's surge rating before condemning the engine.

## What if it's a standby generator that starts then dies?

Standbys add electronics, and the controller usually knows what happened — read the fault code before resetting anything. The portable causes above still apply (especially oil level and air filter), but two standby-specific shutdowns top the list:

- **Low coolant / high temperature.** Liquid-cooled units (typically 20kW and up) will start, run briefly, sense low coolant or a temperature fault, and shut down to protect the engine. Check the reservoir and look for leaks; a low-coolant switch is usually a **$30–80** part, but a real leak needs a tech.
- **Oil pressure fault.** Distinct from the portable's low-oil-*level* sensor: standbys monitor oil *pressure* once running. A failing pressure switch can report low pressure on a healthy engine — or tell the truth about a worn pump or low oil. The controller logs it as an oil pressure fault code. Don't keep resetting and forcing restarts; genuinely low oil pressure destroys an engine in minutes.

Also check the basics: battery, gas valve open, and — on propane — tank level above roughly 10–15%. Repeated unidentified shutdown faults are shop territory: **$100–175** for a diagnostic visit.

## The short version — and when to call a pro

Work it in this order: **oil level first** (dies in seconds), **fuel flow second** — filter, line, and the loose-gas-cap test (dies in a minute or two), **carburetor third** (runs on choke, dies off it), then choke position, spark plug, and air filter. Ninety percent of the starts-then-dies calls I take end at one of those six, most for under $20 in parts.

Call a professional when you've worked the full list and it still dies the same way (you're likely into ignition coil failure, valve problems, or low compression); when a standby throws repeated fault codes; when anything involves gas plumbing or the transfer switch; when you smell strong fuel and can't find the leak; or when the unit is under warranty — most portables carry 2–3 years, standbys around 5, and DIY work can complicate a claim.

A generator that starts is a generator that's close. Fix it this weekend, run it under load for 20 minutes, and store it with stabilized fuel.
