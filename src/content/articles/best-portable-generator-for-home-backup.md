**Quick picks:** The best portable generator for home backup for most households is the **Westinghouse WGen9500DFc** (dual-fuel, 9,500 running watts, 50-amp outlet, typically $1,000–$1,400). The best inverter pick is the **Honda EU3200i** (2,600 running watts, 54–58 dBA, typically $2,400–$3,000 street). The best budget pick is the **WEN GN625i** (5,000 running watts of clean inverter power with 240V output, typically $830–$900). The whole-home-class pick is the **DuroMax XP12000HX** (9,500 running watts, 8.3-gallon tank, typically $1,300–$1,500). Specs below are manufacturer ratings; prices are typical US street bands checked October 2026 — these brands discount constantly, so treat any single price as fiction.

A portable generator is the budget answer to backup power: no permits saga, no $10,000 install, and it can live in the garage between outages. The catch is choosing the right class. Buy too small and the sump pump trips it; buy a loud open-frame when you needed quiet inverter power and you'll hate it by the second night. These four picks cover the four jobs people actually hire a portable to do.

Two numbers decide everything before brand names enter it: **running watts** (what it sustains) versus **starting watts** (the seconds-long surge it survives when motors kick on), and whether the machine makes **inverter power** (clean, electronics-safe, quiet) or conventional open-frame power (cheap watts, ~74 dBA, higher distortion). Size first with our [sizing guide](/articles/what-size-generator-run-house), then pick the class.

## Best dual-fuel: Westinghouse WGen9500DFc

The WGen9500DFc is the default recommendation because it does the whole home-backup job without a weakness that matters. You get **12,500 starting / 9,500 running watts on gasoline** and **11,200 / 8,500 on propane**, a 6.6-gallon tank good for about **8 hours at half load on gas** (up to 12 at quarter load), and the outlet that makes it a home generator rather than a jobsite tool: a **50-amp 14-50R** alongside a 30-amp L14-30R, both ready for an inlet box and interlock or transfer switch. Remote electric start means starting it from the porch in the rain at 2 a.m., and the CO sensor shuts it down if exhaust pools where it shouldn't.

The dual-fuel capability is the strategic feature. Gasoline is the cheap, dense fuel while stations are open; propane is the reserve that stores forever without stabilizer or a gummed carburetor. Run gas first, keep two 20-lb propane tanks as the can't-spoil backup.

The honest limits: at about **74 dBA** and 211+ lbs it's loud and heavy — a wheel-it-out-of-the-garage machine, not a carry-it one — and its power is conventional, not inverter-clean, so sensitive-electronics purists should note the higher THD. Westinghouse backs it with a 3-year limited warranty.

**Best for:** most households — panel-connected backup for essentials plus one big appliance, with propane insurance.

## Best inverter: Honda EU3200i

The EU3200i is what the inverter class is for. **3,200 max / 2,600 rated watts** of clean, stable power, at **54–58 dBA** — quieter than the fridge it will be running — in a 59.1 lb package one person can genuinely carry. Honda's fuel injection (no carburetor to gum up, easier cold starts, better fuel economy) is the real differentiator over cheaper inverters: it runs **8.6 hours at quarter load** on a 1.2-gallon tank, has CO-MINDER shutdown, Bluetooth app monitoring, and parallel capability — two EU3200is link to 6,400 max watts when you outgrow one.

It will run a fridge, furnace blower, lights, router, TV, and a 13,500 BTU RV or window AC — the essentials list, quietly, all night on a gallon and change. That's the outage experience most people actually want.

The honest limits: price and ceiling. Street prices run **$2,400–$3,000** — Honda money — and 2,600 watts is 120V-only essentials territory: no well pump, no central AC, no panel connection without paralleling a second unit. Honda's 3-year residential warranty and dealer network are part of what the premium buys. Its little brother, the EU2200i (2,200/1,800 watts, 48–57 dBA, about 47 lbs, typically $1,100–$1,300), is the pick if 1,800 running watts covers your list.

**Best for:** quiet overnight essentials, electronics safety, apartments-with-a-balcony rules aside — anyone who values sleep and clean power over raw watts.

## Best budget: WEN GN625i

The GN625i breaks the class rules, which is why it's here: **6,250 surge / 5,000 rated watts of inverter power with 120/240V output** at a street price around **$830–$900**. Inverter-clean output (WEN rates THD under 0.5% at no load) plus a transfer-switch-ready L14-30R outlet plus an RV TT-30 — at this price, that combination used to not exist. The 4.2-gallon tank runs about **8.1 hours at half load**, eco-mode throttles the engine down for light loads, and at roughly 110–120 lbs with the wheel kit it's manageable.

That wattage covers the honest essentials list with surge headroom: fridge, furnace blower, sump pump, lights, router, and a microwave in bursts. It's the machine I point people to when the budget is firm and the loads are real.

The honest limits: it's an open-frame inverter, so it's quieter than a conventional open-frame but not Honda-quiet; it's gasoline-only (no propane reserve); and WEN's warranty is 2 years against Honda's 3 and the big frames' 3. The GN625iX variant adds a CO shutdown sensor — worth the small premium where it's offered.

**Best for:** firm budgets under $900 that still need 240V and electronics-safe power.

## Best whole-home class: DuroMax XP12000HX

When "essentials" stops being the brief — you want the AC, the well pump, and the water heater in rotation — the XP12000HX is the portable that plays in that league: **12,000 peak / 9,500 running watts on gasoline** and **11,400 / 9,025 on propane**, an 8.3-gallon tank for about **8.8 hours at half load** (7.2 hours on a 40-lb propane tank), a 50-amp outlet for panel connection, all-copper windings, CO Alert shutdown, and push-button electric start. DuroMax backs it with a 3-year limited warranty, and it lists around **$1,499**, with street prices typically $1,300–$1,500.

Against the Westinghouse above, the DuroMax trades remote start for a bigger tank and longer legs; both are legitimate whole-home-class answers at nearly the same money. Pick the Westinghouse for the fob and EFI variants, the DuroMax for runtime.

The honest limits: same class truths — about 74 dBA, 200+ lbs, conventional (not inverter) power, and manual everything. It powers most of a house; it does not *manage* a house. For automatic, walk-away coverage, compare what you're really asking for against [standby generator cost](/articles/standby-generator-cost) before spending up.

**Best for:** large homes, wells and AC in the load list, and buyers who accept manual operation in exchange for standby-class watts at a tenth of the installed price.

## How do you connect a portable to your house safely?

Whichever pick you buy, the connection matters more than the brand. The legal, safe routes are a **power inlet box with an interlock kit** or a **transfer switch**, installed by an electrician for roughly $600–$1,500 depending on method — our [guide to connecting a generator to your house](/articles/how-to-connect-a-generator-to-your-house) compares all three legal methods with costs. The illegal route — a male-to-male "suicide cord" into a dryer outlet — backfeeds the grid, can kill line workers, and voids your insurance. Never.

<div class="safety"><strong>Safety note:</strong> Every generator in this guide burns fuel and makes carbon monoxide. Run it outdoors, at least 20 feet from the house, exhaust pointed away from windows — never in a garage, even with the door open. CO is odorless; the sensors on these machines are a backstop, not a plan.</div>

## Portable or standby: which is the honest buy?

Portables win on price by an order of magnitude: $830–$1,500 buys any machine in this guide, versus $9,500–$15,000 installed for a standby system. Standbys win on everything else — automatic start in 10 seconds, no refueling in the storm, no 2 a.m. trip to the garage, fuel from the gas line instead of a can. The deciding questions are outage length and who's home: frequent multi-day outages, medical equipment, or an empty house half the year argue for standby (see the [whole-house cost guide](/articles/whole-house-generator-cost)); a few outages a year with someone home to roll the machine out argues for everything on this page. And if noise is your deciding factor across all generators, our [quietest generators guide](/articles/quietest-generators-noise-levels) ranks the whole field by verified decibels. Even a loud open-frame model can be tamed noticeably with placement, barriers, and muffler choices, which our guide on [how to make a generator quiet](/articles/how-to-make-a-generator-quiet) covers step by step.
