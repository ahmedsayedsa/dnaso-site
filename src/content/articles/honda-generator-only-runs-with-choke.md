Your Honda generator only runs with choke on. It starts fine — Hondas usually do — purrs along while the choke lever is out, and the second you push the choke in like you're supposed to, it stumbles, sags, and dies.

Here's what I tell every customer: your generator is giving you a clean diagnosis for free. A Honda that only runs on choke is an engine that isn't getting enough fuel, and the choke is the crutch it's leaning on. In 18 years of working on these machines, nine times out of ten the cause is one tiny clogged jet in the carburetor — usually after the unit sat in storage with last season's gas in it.

Before anything else, three safety rules:

1. **Work on a cool engine, exhaust included.** The EU muffler sits beside the carburetor and stays burn-you hot long after shutdown — give it 15–20 minutes.
2. **Kill the fuel and spark before wrenching.** Fuel valve OFF, engine switch OFF, spark plug cap pulled. You'll be working with open fuel lines and a bowl of gasoline.
3. **Handle fuel carefully.** Outdoors, no smoking, no pilot lights nearby, drain fuel into an approved can only.

## What Does It Mean When a Generator Only Runs on Choke?

An engine wants roughly 14.7 parts air to 1 part fuel by weight. A cold engine needs a richer mix to start, so the choke plate closes off most of the air entering the carburetor. Less air across the same fuel equals a richer mixture. That's all the choke is: an air restrictor.

Now flip the logic. If your warmed-up engine **needs** that restriction to keep running, then with the choke open the mixture is too **lean** — too much air, or (far more likely) too little fuel. The choke isn't fixing anything; it's throttling air down to match a weak fuel supply. Every cause below does one of two things:

- **Cuts fuel flow** — clogged jet, stale-fuel varnish, blocked filter, weak pump.
- **Adds unmetered air** — a leak at a carb gasket or intake joint.

## How Can You Confirm It's Running Lean? The Half-Choke Test

Before you remove a single screw, run this two-minute test. Warm the generator up two or three minutes on choke, then push the choke in slowly and watch each position — on most EU-series Hondas you can hold the lever between detents with your finger.

- **Dies at full open, runs smoothly at half choke, runs rich at full choke** — classic fuel restriction. Half choke is exactly compensating for fuel the carburetor should be delivering.
- **Runs fine at full open once genuinely warm (5+ minutes)** — maybe not a fault at all. Some small Hondas are jetted lean from the factory and genuinely cold-blooded.
- **Dies the instant you move the choke at all, even warm** — the blockage is severe. These often overlap with machines that [start and then die within seconds](/articles/generator-starts-then-dies/).
- **Runs better under load than at idle** — another fingerprint: a clogged pilot jet starves the idle circuit worst, so the engine smooths out when a load opens the throttle and the main jet takes over, then falls apart when the load drops.
- **Moving the choke changes nothing** — check the linkage is actually moving the plate before blaming mixture at all.

## Is It a Clogged Pilot Jet? (The Usual Suspect)

Yes, until proven otherwise. The carburetor on a Honda EU2000i or EU2200i is a marvel of miniaturization, and that's the problem: tiny engines (79cc and 121cc) sipping fuel through jets sized for sipping. The **pilot jet** (the idle or slow jet) meters fuel for idle and low RPM through an opening a fraction of a millimeter wide; the **main jet**, at the bottom of the emulsion tube, is bigger but still small. A film of oxidized fuel that a lawn tractor carb would shrug off can half-close a Honda pilot jet — and a half-closed pilot jet passes enough fuel to run *with* choke and not enough to run without it. Precisely your symptom.

The timeline is almost always the same: it ran perfectly in October, then sat all winter with E10 in the tank and bowl. The ethanol absorbed moisture, the light fractions evaporated, and varnish plated every small passage, starting with the smallest. Storage history plus the half-choke test plus improvement under load equals pilot jet — I'll bet the service call on it.

## Could It Just Be Stale Fuel?

Stale fuel and a clogged jet are usually two chapters of the same story, but sometimes you get lucky: the fuel in the bowl has gone off while the passages are still clean. Fuel loses volatility in as little as 30 days, and by six months unstabilized E10 smells like varnish and burns lean.

**The fresh-fuel trial costs nothing, so do it first.** Shut the valve, drain the carb bowl (small drain screw at the bottom on EU carbs), drain or siphon the tank, and refill with fresh gasoline bought this week, stabilizer added. Give it five minutes to purge the old fuel. If it now runs cleanly off choke, you just saved yourself a teardown — don't waste the luck: make stabilized fuel (or running the bowl dry) your storage routine. The [generator maintenance checklist](/articles/generator-maintenance-checklist/) has the full seasonal version. If fresh fuel changes nothing, the deposits are already in the jets. Move on.

## What About the Fuel Filter and Sediment?

More chokepoints sit between the tank and that tiny pilot jet:

- **The tank outlet screen.** EU-series Hondas have a fine strainer at the tank outlet or filler neck. It traps rust and grit from old tanks and clogs gradually — the choke habit creeps up over a season.
- **The inline filter or sediment cup**, where fitted. Some Honda engines (more on open-frame EM/EB models than EU inverters) use a sediment bowl under the fuel valve. Unscrew it: often a spoonful of rust, water, and gray sludge.
- **The fuel line itself.** Ethanol eats older rubber lines from inside; one that looks fine outside can shed black crumbs into the carb inlet or collapse internally.

**The check:** Disconnect the line at the carb inlet (valve open, container ready). Fuel should flow in a strong, steady stream — not a drip, not a pulse. Weak flow? Work backward: filter, screen, line, valve. Parts run **$5–15**. Fix flow *before* blaming the carb — cleaning one and feeding it through a dirty filter schedules the same repair twice.

## Could It Be an Air Leak Instead?

Less common than a clog, but the identical symptom by the opposite route: instead of too little fuel, the engine gets **extra air** downstream of the carburetor, leaning the mixture no matter how much fuel the jets deliver. Suspects: the gaskets and insulator between carburetor and head, mounting nuts backed off by vibration, cracks in the plastic intake spacer.

**The check:** With the engine running on choke, spray short bursts of carb cleaner around the carb-to-engine joint — *not* into the throat, and sparingly; it's flammable near a hot engine. If RPM jumps or the engine smooths out, spray got sucked through a leak and briefly enriched the mixture. Found it. Fix: a gasket set (**$8–15**), surfaces cleaned flat, nuts snugged evenly — overtightening warps the flange and creates the leak you're fixing.

## What About Fuel Pumps and Altitude?

Two causes that rarely apply, quickly. **Fuel pump:** larger Hondas may use a vacuum-operated pump driven by crankcase pulses. A torn diaphragm or cracked pulse hose delivers *some* fuel — enough to fill the bowl for starting — then falls behind as demand rises, and off choke the engine starves. The tells: it runs off choke a minute or two then fades, or inlet flow is a lazy pulse instead of a stream. Kits run **$15–35**. EU inverters don't have one — gravity is simple, and simple is good.

**Altitude:** one footnote — an engine fitted with Honda's high-altitude jets (smaller main jet, for use above roughly 5,000 feet) and then run at sea level **can** end up lean and choke-dependent, since those jets under-fuel in dense air. If yours came from a mountain town, check what's in the carb before cleaning it a third time.

## Will Carb Cleaner in the Tank Fix It?

No, and I want to be straight with you because the bottles promise otherwise. Additives soften light, fresh varnish in a carb that's still flowing reasonably — that's their job. But your pilot jet isn't coated, it's *plugged*, with deposits hardened into a hole a fraction of a millimeter wide. Fuel barely flows through it at idle; if fuel barely flows, cleaner barely flows, and it can't dissolve a plug it can't reach. I've cleaned carbs for customers who ran three bottles through at $9 a bottle — the plug was still there. Save the $9: the carb comes apart, or it gets replaced.

## How Do You Clean an EU-Series Carburetor, Step by Step?

This covers the EU2000i/EU2200i class of inverter. Open-frame Hondas differ in detail but not in logic. Budget **45–75 minutes** the first time. Tools: Phillips and flat screwdrivers, 10mm socket, needle-nose pliers, carb cleaner with the straw, compressed air if you have it, one strand of fine copper wire (a wire-brush bristle works), a muffin tin for parts. Photograph every step before disconnecting anything — linkage routing is the part everyone forgets.

1. **Prep and access.** Fuel valve OFF. Drain the bowl via the drain screw. Spark plug cap off. On EU models, remove the side cover and carb-side panel screws, then pull the foam air filter and unbolt the airbox (screws and a breather hose) — the carb sits behind it.
2. **Disconnect.** Photograph the throttle linkage and governor spring, then unhook them. Slide the fuel line off the inlet.
3. **Unbolt the carburetor.** Two nuts (typically 10mm) on studs through the intake insulator. Keep the gasket stack in order — engine-side gasket, insulator, carb-side gasket — and mark the insulator's orientation.
4. **Strip it on the tray.** Drop the float bowl (one bolt); the float pivots on a pin that slides out, needle valve hanging from its tab. Unscrew the **main jet** — brass, at the bottom of the central tower, slotted; use a screwdriver that fits, the slot chews easily. The **emulsion tube** sits directly above it. The **pilot jet** is a small brass jet in the upper body near the idle circuit — on many EU carbs under a plastic cap or beside the idle screw. Press-in jets can be coaxed out with pliers or cleaned in place.
5. **Clean everything.** Spray cleaner through every passage from both directions until it exits somewhere else. Hold each jet to the light: you want a clean, round pinhole. Work the copper strand through the main jet and the emulsion tube's tiny cross-holes — all of them matter. **Never** use a steel bit; a jet enlarged 10% is a permanently rich generator.
6. **Reassemble in reverse.** New gaskets if the old ones tore. Float height is fixed by the tab — confirm the needle seats and the float moves freely.
7. **Test.** Fresh stabilized fuel, valve on, two minutes for the bowl to fill. Start on choke, warm two minutes, choke off. A correctly cleaned EU settles into a steady idle; with Eco-Throttle on it loafs quietly, revs crisply when you plug in a load (a space heater is a good test), and settles back when you unplug it. No hunting, no choke — that's your proof: Eco-Throttle only idles down cleanly when the idle circuit actually feeds the engine.

## Should You Clean the Carburetor or Just Replace It?

- **Clean the original:** $10–15 in cleaner and gaskets, about an hour, and you keep the genuine Honda carb — a genuinely good part.
- **Aftermarket replacement:** **$25–40** for an EU2000i/EU2200i clone, 20 minutes to swap. My unvarnished take: the clones are a lottery. Many run great out of the box; a meaningful minority have jets drilled slightly off, idle circuits that never settle, or float needles that stick within a season — and you'll chase a *new* weird symptom instead of fixing the old one. If you go clone, keep the original in a bag; it's cleanable later, and you may clean it.
- **OEM Honda carburetor:** **$100 or more**, and it works exactly like the original did, because it is the original.

My rule: clean first — the deposit is the problem, not the part. Go OEM if the carb body is corroded or a proper cleaning doesn't restore it.

## When Should You Call a Professional Instead?

The choke symptom is carburetor-deep — homeowner territory with basic tools. Hand it off when:

- **You cleaned it properly and it still needs choke.** Something structural is going on — a worn throttle shaft leaking air, a warped flange, low compression — needing measurement, not more cleaning. If it now refuses to start at all, work the [generator won't start checklist](/articles/generator-wont-start-troubleshooting/) first.
- **It hunts and stalls under load even after the fix.** That overlaps with the governor and output issues in our [generator surging guide](/articles/generator-surging/).
- **It's under warranty.** A choke complaint inside the warranty window is the dealer's problem, not yours.
- **You found fuel leaking anywhere.** Fuel leaks on a hot engine are where DIY ends.

A shop typically charges **$120–200** all-in — you're buying the hour and the certainty.
