If you want one number: most American homes land between **$8,000 and $15,000 fully installed** for an air-cooled standby generator, and the most common quotes I see cluster around **$10,000 to $12,500**. The generator unit itself is roughly half the bill. The other half is electrician labor, the gas line, the transfer switch, the pad, and permits — and that half is where quotes vary the most.

This guide breaks down every line of that bill so you can read a quote the way an electrician reads it: and spot the padding.

## The short answer: total installed cost by home size

| Home | Typical generator size | Total installed range |
|---|---|---|
| Small (1,200–1,800 sq ft), gas heat, no central AC | 10–14 kW | $8,000–$10,500 |
| Average (1,800–2,800 sq ft), central AC | 14–20 kW | $10,000–$13,000 |
| Large (2,800–4,000 sq ft), 4–5 ton AC | 22–26 kW | $12,500–$16,000 |
| Large home, electric heat or pool/well pump | 26 kW+ or liquid-cooled | $16,000–$25,000+ |

These are air-cooled units running on natural gas or propane. Liquid-cooled models start around $20,000 installed and climb fast — more on that below.

## What the unit itself costs

The standby market is dominated by **Generac** (Guardian series), **Kohler**, **Cummins**, and **Champion**, with **Briggs & Stratton** and **Westinghouse** in the mix. Air-cooled models all use V-twin engines running at 3,600 RPM, and pricing tracks size pretty predictably:

| Size | Typical unit price (2026) | Powers |
|---|---|---|
| 10 kW | $3,500–$4,500 | Essentials: fridge, furnace blower, lights, sump, some outlets |
| 14 kW | $4,000–$5,000 | Typical smaller home with gas heat; often with load management for one AC |
| 18 kW | $4,800–$5,800 | Mid-size home, 3-ton AC possible |
| 20–22 kW | $5,500–$6,500 | Most common "whole house" size for 2,000–3,000 sq ft homes |
| 24–26 kW | $6,000–$7,500 | Large homes, 4–5 ton AC, electric water heater |
| 36–48 kW (liquid-cooled) | $12,000–$20,000+ | Large or all-electric homes; runs quieter, lasts longer |

A Generac Guardian 22kW and a Kohler 20RCA sit in the same bracket — the brand matters less than the size and the installer. Online prices look tempting, but dealers price units as part of the installed package, and most manufacturers' best warranties require dealer installation.

Note that "whole house" rarely means literally everything. Most installs use **load management**: smart modules ($200–$400 each installed) that shed the AC or water heater for the seconds the generator needs to stabilize, instead of buying a bigger generator. Two $300 modules can save you $2,000 on a larger unit. A good load calculation — not a guess — decides your size. Ask for it in writing.

## Installation: the other half of the bill

A standby generator isn't a plug-in appliance. Getting it running takes an electrician, a gas fitter, and usually a permit. Typical installation labor runs **$3,000–$6,000** total, and it breaks down like this:

**Electrical work: $1,500–$3,000.** This is the biggest labor chunk. It includes mounting and wiring the automatic transfer switch (ATS), running conduit and conductors between the generator, the ATS, and your main panel, landing the control wiring, and the startup/commissioning sequence (most brands require a documented startup for the warranty). If your panel is full, far from the generator site, or needs reorganization, this climbs.

**Gas line: $500–$2,000+.** The generator needs a dedicated gas line sized for its full-load BTU draw — a 22kW unit pulls roughly 280,000–330,000 BTU/hr at full load, which is a lot of pipe. Short runs (under 20 feet) from the meter are cheap. Long runs across the house, upsizing the meter, or switching to propane with a new tank add real money. Propane tank installation alone can run $1,000–$2,500 for the tank, pad, and first fill.

**Concrete pad or base: $300–$800.** The unit needs a level, stable base. Many models now ship with a composite mounting pad that sits on compacted gravel ($0–$200), but a poured concrete pad is still standard in many areas and for larger units.

**Permits and inspections: $100–$500.** Most jurisdictions require an electrical permit, and many require a separate gas/mechanical permit. Some HOAs and cities add their own review. Budget $100–$500 total; the installer usually pulls these.

**Miscellaneous: $200–$600.** Battery (often included), cold-weather kit in northern climates ($200–$400 — worth it if you see sub-zero temperatures), extended exercise/maintenance kit, gravel, paint for conduit.

## What drives the price up or down

Five things move a quote more than anything else:

**1. Gas line run distance.** This is the single biggest swing factor. A 10-foot run off an existing meter stub might cost $500. A 60-foot run with trenching, a meter upgrade from the gas utility, and a second-stage regulator can hit $2,500–$3,500. Where the generator sits relative to your meter matters enormously — placement is a cost decision, not just an aesthetic one.

**2. Your electrical panel.** If you have a modern 200-amp panel with space, the ATS goes in cleanly. If you're on an old 100-amp service, a fuse box, a Zinsco or Federal Pacific panel, or your panel is stuffed, the electrician has to upgrade or rework it first. A panel upgrade alone runs $2,000–$4,000, and it can blindside a project.

**3. Setback and placement rules.** Code and manufacturer specs dictate placement: typically **18 inches from the house wall and 5 feet from windows, doors, and fresh-air intakes** (NFPA 37 and manufacturer requirements — Generac, Kohler, and Cummins all publish nearly identical clearances). If your yard layout forces the unit far from the panel or the meter, every extra foot is wire and pipe you pay for. Trenching through rock or concrete adds more.

**4. Local labor rates.** The same job costs meaningfully more in New Jersey or California than in Tennessee or Ohio. Labor is 40–60% of most installs, so regional rates move the total more than the unit price does.

**5. Smart load management vs. upsizing.** As noted above, a couple of load-shed modules can let a 22kW do the job of a 26kW. A contractor who prices the bigger unit without running the load calc is either lazy or padding the sale.

<div class="safety"><strong>Safety note:</strong> Gas connections must be installed by a licensed plumber or gas fitter and permitted/inspected in your jurisdiction. An undersized or leaking gas line is a carbon monoxide and fire hazard — this is never a DIY line item.</div>

## Anatomy of a $12,500 quote

Here's what a fair, middle-of-the-road $12,500 installed quote for a 22kW air-cooled unit on a 2,400 sq ft home typically contains:

| Line item | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| 22kW generator + ATS bundle | $6,200 |
| Electrical labor (ATS, conduit, wiring, startup) | $2,400 |
| Gas line (25-ft run, meter verification) | $1,100 |
| Concrete pad + gravel base | $550 |
| Permits + inspection fees | $350 |
| Two load-management modules | $600 |
| Cold-weather kit | $300 |
| **Total** | **$11,500–$12,500** |

If a quote doesn't break things out roughly like this, ask why. Vague "turnkey installed" line items are where padding hides.

## What the quote might not include (but should)

A lowball quote sometimes looks low because it quietly omits things you'll pay for anyway. Before comparing quotes, confirm each one covers:

- **Gas meter upgrade fees.** If your meter can't supply the generator's full-load BTU draw plus the house, the gas utility upgrades it — sometimes free, sometimes $200–$600. Ask who pays and who coordinates it.
- **Propane tank costs.** On propane, clarify whether the quote includes the tank, the pad under it, trenching to the house, and the first fill — or whether you're renting the tank from the propane company ($50–$150/year rental is common).
- **Panel upgrades.** If the site visit reveals a full panel or an obsolete brand, that $2,000–$4,000 upgrade lands on top of the generator quote. Get it priced upfront, not mid-install.
- **Trenching restoration.** Digging a 40-foot trench for the gas line tears up the yard. Some installers backfill and reseed; some leave you with a dirt scar. Ask.
- **Warranty registration and documentation.** The installer should register the warranty, hand you the startup report, and show you the exercise schedule and app setup. If they shrug at this, their after-install support will match.

Two quotes $2,000 apart aren't really $2,000 apart if the cheaper one omits the gas work and the permits. Compare line items, not totals.

## Air-cooled vs. liquid-cooled: the price jump

Air-cooled units (everything up to 26kW) are the standard residential choice. Liquid-cooled models — 36kW and up, car-style engines running at 1,800 RPM — cost **$12,000–$20,000+ for the unit** and $20,000–$35,000+ installed. They run quieter, last 10,000+ hours (see our [lifespan guide](/articles/how-long-do-generators-last)), and suit large or all-electric homes with heavy, sustained loads.

For most homes, liquid-cooled is overkill. If a contractor pushes you toward one for a 2,500 sq ft gas-heated house, get a second opinion — that's a red flag, not an upgrade.

## How to not get ripped off

**Get three quotes, and make them line-item quotes.** Any reputable dealer will break out unit, ATS, electrical, gas, pad, and permits. If someone won't itemize, move on.

**Demand a load calculation.** Sizing by "square footage rules of thumb" is how you end up with an oversized unit. A proper calc lists your actual loads (AC tonnage, well pump, water heater, etc.) and shows the math.

**Insist on a site visit before the final number.** Phone quotes are estimates at best. The gas run distance, panel condition, and placement constraints can only be priced on-site.

**Watch for these red flags:** a quote with no site visit; a single lump-sum "installed" figure with no breakdown; pressure to sign same-day for a "discount"; upselling liquid-cooled for a standard home; and anyone who says permits "aren't needed" — they almost always are.

**Check the warranty terms.** Most brands offer 5-year limited warranties; 7–10 year extended warranties are common add-ons ($500–$1,500). Confirm the warranty requires dealer installation and documented startup — and that your installer actually registers it.

## Financing

Most dealers offer financing through partners like Synchrony or manufacturer programs — typical terms run 6.99–12.99% APR over 5–10 years, which puts a $12,000 install around **$135–$240/month**. Home equity lines are usually cheaper if you have the equity. Some utilities and states offer rebates or tax incentives for standby power, though these change often — check your state's energy office before you buy.

## The bottom line

Budget **$10,000–$13,000** for a typical whole-house install, get three itemized quotes, and pay the most attention to the gas and electrical line items — that's where the real money moves. A well-installed 22kW air-cooled unit will run your home for decades of normal outage use.
