Power goes out. It's 9pm, the fridge is warming up, the kids are asking questions you don't want to answer, and your phone is at 12%. Every homeowner hits that moment and thinks: *I should have handled this already.*

This guide is the whole picture. Generator types, batteries, sizing math, real installed costs, fuel, transfer switches, maintenance — everything you need to pick the right backup power setup for your house, without paying for more than you need.

## Why backup power isn't just for preppers anymore

Outages are getting longer and more common. Storms knock out neighborhoods for days, not hours. Heat waves strain the grid right when you need AC most. A sump pump that stops for six hours during a storm can cost you a flooded basement — a $5,000–$10,000 problem. A freezer full of food is a few hundred dollars gone in 48 hours. For people on medical equipment — CPAP machines, oxygen concentrators, refrigerated medication — it's not a convenience issue at all.

Here's the thing most articles skip: **backup power is really about which loads matter to you.** A $600 portable generator running extension cords to your fridge, a few lights, and your phone charger is backup power. A $12,000 standby unit wired into your breaker panel that kicks on by itself is also backup power. The right answer depends on what you can't lose.

## The three generator types (plus batteries)

There are really four ways to keep your house powered when the grid dies. Each has a clear job.

### 1. Portable generators — $500 to $1,500

The classic. A gas engine on a steel frame with wheels, bought at a big-box store. A unit like the **Champion 100520** (8,750 starting watts) or **Westinghouse WGen7500** runs roughly $800–$1,000. The **Generac GP6500** — probably the most common portable in America — sits around $800–$900.

**What they do:** Power your essentials through heavy-duty extension cords, or a limited set of circuits through a manual transfer switch (more on that later). You wheel it out, fill it with gas, pull the cord (or push a button on electric-start models), and plug in.

**The honest pros:**
- Cheapest way to get real power ($500–$1,500 for 5,000–9,500 watts)
- No installation — buy it today, use it tonight
- Useful for other jobs: job sites, RVs, tailgates

**The honest cons:**
- **You have to be home.** Nobody's dragging a generator out at 3am when the outage hits while you're asleep or at work.
- Fuel logistics. A 7,500-watt portable burns roughly **0.75–1 gallon of gasoline per hour** at half load. A 5-gallon tank gets you 6–8 hours. A three-day outage means 20+ gallons of gas — stored safely somewhere, bought before the storm empties the gas stations.
- Noise. Open-frame portables run 72–78 decibels — lawnmower territory. Your neighbors will know.
- Manual everything: refueling, starting, shutting down at night.

**Best for:** Short outages (hours, not days), tight budgets, people who are home most of the time and don't mind a little hassle.

### 2. Inverter generators — $1,000 to $2,500

Inverter generators are a different design — they produce cleaner power (low total harmonic distortion, safe for electronics) and throttle the engine down when the load is light, which saves fuel and cuts noise.

A **Honda EU2200i** (~$1,100) or **Yamaha EF2200iS** (~$1,000) gives you 2,200 starting watts — enough for a fridge, lights, phones, a laptop, and a small window AC. Bigger inverter units like the **Champion 100520** or **Westinghouse iGen9500DF** push into the 7,500–9,500 watt range at $1,500–$2,500.

**The honest pros:**
- Quiet — 52–62 dB, roughly conversation level
- Fuel-efficient: a 2,200-watt inverter sips ~0.1 gal/hour at light load and runs 8+ hours on a gallon
- Clean power for sensitive electronics (CPAP machines, computers, modern furnace controls)

**The honest cons:**
- Cost per watt is higher than open-frame portables
- Smaller units can't touch big loads like central AC or electric ranges
- Still manual — same "you have to be there" limitation

**Best for:** People who mainly need to keep the essentials alive quietly — fridge, phones, laptops, a CPAP, maybe a window AC. Excellent for apartments and townhouses where noise matters.

### 3. Standby (whole-house) generators — $8,000 to $15,000 installed

This is the permanent solution. A weatherproof unit bolted to a concrete pad next to your house, wired directly into your electrical panel through an automatic transfer switch (ATS), fueled by your home's natural gas line or a propane tank.

The market leaders are **Generac Guardian series** (the 22kW Guardian is the best-selling home standby in the US, ~$5,500–$6,500 for the unit alone) and **Kohler 20RCA** (~$5,500–$6,000 unit). **Cummins** and **Briggs & Stratton** round out the field.

**What happens during an outage:** The ATS senses the power loss, signals the generator, and your house is back on power in about 10–30 seconds. You might not even be home. It runs until utility power returns, then switches back and shuts itself down.

**The honest pros:**
- Fully automatic — works during work hours, vacations, 3am outages
- Runs on natural gas (no refueling, no gas-station runs) or propane
- Powers the whole house, including central AC
- Runs a weekly self-test so it doesn't sit dead for a year

**The honest cons:**
- **Cost.** Installed, you're looking at $8,000–$15,000 depending on size, fuel work, and local labor. In expensive markets (California, Northeast), $15,000–$18,000 isn't unusual.
- Fuel consumption. A 22kW standby at full load burns about **2.5–3.5 gallons of propane per hour** or roughly 300+ cubic feet of natural gas per hour. Your gas bill will notice a multi-day outage.
- Needs maintenance: oil changes, air filters, spark plugs — annually at minimum (~$200–$400/year if you hire it out).
- Not silent — 60–68 dB, about like a car idling. Quieter than a portable, but your neighbors hear it.

**Best for:** Homes where outages are frequent or long, people with medical equipment, anyone who travels, sump-pump country, and anyone who wants zero hassle.

### 4. Home battery backup — $10,000 to $18,000 installed

Battery systems like the **Tesla Powerwall 3** (13.5 kWh, ~$9,000–$11,000 before install), **EcoFlow Delta Pro Ultra** (6 kWh per battery, expandable), or **Enphase IQ Battery** store electricity and discharge it during outages — silently, with no fuel, no exhaust.

**The honest pros:**
- Silent. No fuel. No emissions. No refueling runs.
- Instant switchover (milliseconds — your computer doesn't even blink)
- Great paired with solar: the panels recharge the battery during the day

**The honest cons:**
- **Capacity is the limit.** One 13.5 kWh Powerwall runs a fridge, lights, phones, and Wi-Fi for maybe 12–18 hours. Add central AC and you're down to 2–3 hours. Multi-day outages need solar or multiple batteries — and the price climbs fast.
- Output power is limited. A single Powerwall 3 delivers 11.5 kW continuous — enough for most homes, but large ACs and electric ranges can trip it.
- Batteries degrade. Expect meaningful capacity loss over 10–15 years.

**Best for:** Short outages (under a day), homes with solar, neighborhoods where generator noise or HOA rules are an issue, and people who want silent, maintenance-free backup for the essentials.

## Sizing: the math that actually matters

This is where people mess up. Generator sizing isn't about square footage — it's about watts. And there are two kinds of watts you need to know.

### Running watts vs. starting watts

**Running watts** = what an appliance draws while it's operating. Your fridge hums along at 150–200 watts.

**Starting watts** = the brief surge when a motor kicks on. That same fridge needs about 1,200 watts for the first second or two while the compressor starts.

The rule: **your generator's starting-watt rating must cover the largest surge in your setup, and its running-watt rating must cover everything running at once.**

### The sizing process in 5 minutes

1. List what you want to power.
2. Add up the **running** watts.
3. Find the appliance with the biggest **starting** surge. Add the difference between its starting and running watts to your total.
4. Add **20% headroom** on top. Generators last longer and run more efficiently when they're not maxed out.

Example: fridge (200 running / 1,200 starting) + 6 LED lights (60) + Wi-Fi + phone chargers (50) + furnace blower (800 running / 2,300 starting) + sump pump (900 running / 2,000 starting).

- Running total: 200 + 60 + 50 + 800 + 900 = **2,010 W**
- Biggest surge: furnace blower (2,300 − 800 = 1,500 extra)
- Sizing target: (2,010 + 1,500) × 1.2 = **~4,200 W**

So a 5,500-watt portable covers this comfortably. That's the method — it works for any house.

<div class="safety"><strong>Safety note:</strong> Never run a generator in a garage, basement, or any enclosed space — carbon monoxide kills within minutes and you cannot smell it. Keep portables at least 20 feet from the house with the exhaust pointed away from windows and doors.</div>

### Why oversizing wastes fuel (and money)

A 22kW standby powering a 4kW load is like driving a V8 truck to get groceries — it works, but it drinks fuel to do it. Generators are most efficient at 50–80% load. A standby idling at 20% load still burns a meaningful amount of fuel per hour. Buy the size your math says, plus 20% headroom. Not double.

## Fuel types: pick by logistics, not theory

- **Gasoline** — available everywhere, goes stale in 3–6 months without stabilizer (use Sta-Bil or equivalent). You'll be refueling every 6–8 hours on a portable. During a regional outage, gas stations run dry or lose power themselves.
- **Propane** — never goes stale, stores indefinitely. A 20-lb grill tank runs a 7,500-watt portable ~4–5 hours. Standbys on a 250–500 gallon buried tank can run for days. Downside: propane has less energy per gallon, and big tanks need installation.
- **Natural gas** — the standby gold standard. No refueling, no storage, no stale fuel. Limitation: if your area doesn't have gas service, it's not an option, and in rare catastrophic events gas pressure can drop.
- **Diesel** — great for large commercial units; overkill for most homes, and diesel gel in cold weather is its own project.
- **Dual-fuel / tri-fuel** — many portables (Champion, Westinghouse, Firman) run on gasoline and propane. Nice flexibility: start on gasoline, switch to propane for long outages.

## Transfer switches: the part people skip

If you plug extension cords into a portable, you don't need one. But the moment you want the generator feeding your breaker panel, you need a transfer switch. This is non-negotiable and it's about safety, not convenience.

**Manual transfer switch** ($300–$600 installed, plus the switch itself ~$200–$400): you flip a lever to disconnect from the grid and connect the generator. Required by electrical code. Without it, your generator can **backfeed power onto the grid** — energizing downed lines and putting utility lineworkers' lives at risk.

**Automatic transfer switch** ($500–$1,500 as part of a standby install): does the same thing in seconds, with no human involved.

<div class="safety"><strong>Safety note:</strong> Backfeeding a generator through a dryer outlet or any "suicide cord" is illegal in most jurisdictions and can kill a utility worker repairing lines during an outage. Use a proper transfer switch or interlock kit installed by an electrician. This is the one shortcut that can get someone killed.</div>

## Maintenance: the boring part that decides whether it works

A generator that sits for two years and then gets started in a storm has about a 50/50 chance of working. Here's the minimum:

- **Portable:** run it 15–20 minutes every 2–3 months under light load. Change oil after the first 25 hours, then every 50–100 hours. Use fuel stabilizer in anything you store; run the carburetor dry before storage. Check the air filter yearly.
- **Standby:** runs a weekly 5–10 minute self-test automatically. Still needs annual oil, filter, and spark plug service — DIY for ~$60 in parts or $200–$400 for a service contract. Budget this. A dead standby is a $12,000 lawn ornament.
- **Batteries:** almost nothing. Check the app occasionally, keep the firmware updated.

Don't forget the ongoing costs: fuel, annual maintenance, and for portables, the value of your time standing in a gas station line during a storm.

## The decision framework: if X, get Y

- **Outages are rare and short, budget under $1,500:** Portable generator (Generac GP6500, Champion 100520) + heavy-duty extension cords. Start here; add a manual transfer switch later.
- **You need quiet and mainly cover essentials:** Inverter generator. A 2,200-watt Honda or Yamaha handles fridge + lights + electronics. Step up to a 7,500+ watt inverter if you want more headroom.
- **Frequent or multi-day outages, medical equipment, you travel, sump pump, or you just want it automatic:** Standby generator, 14–22 kW depending on your sizing math. Natural gas if you have it.
- **Short outages, solar on the roof, noise/HOA restrictions, or you hate maintenance:** Home battery. Size for your essentials, not the whole house — that keeps the price sane.
- **You have solar AND worry about long outages:** Battery + solar is the combo that actually gets better the longer the outage lasts. The generator crowd refuels; you recharge.
