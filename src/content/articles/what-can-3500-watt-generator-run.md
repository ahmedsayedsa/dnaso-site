"What can a 3500 watt generator run?" is the question I get from homeowners who priced the 2,000-watt inverters, realized a window AC wasn't in that world, and don't want to pay for 5,000 watts they'll never use. Fair instinct — 3,500 watts is the smallest size that runs a real house's essentials *plus* one comfort load that matters: a window AC in summer, or the furnace blower and a space-heated room in winter. What it won't do is forgive sloppy stacking. The difference between happy owners and frustrated ones is almost never the generator. It's whether somebody did the load math before the storm. So let's do it.

**Quick answer:** A 3,500-watt generator runs a refrigerator, freezer, sump pump, furnace blower, lights, and internet — about **2,100 watts of essentials** — plus one window AC (900–1,200W) or a microwave in rotation. It cannot run an electric water heater, an electric dryer, central AC, or a 1 HP well pump's startup surge.

## Is that 3,500 watts running or starting?

Read the box carefully, because this class contains two different machines wearing the same number. A conventional open-frame "3500" is usually a true **3,500 running watts with about 4,375 starting watts.** But the popular inverter in this class — Harbor Freight's Predator 3500 — is rated the other way: **3,500 starting, 3,000 running.** Both are good machines; they're not the same machine. Everything in this article is about the 3,500-running-watt class. If you're buying the inverter, scale every combo below down by about 15% — its quietness and fuel sipping are the compensation.

The two numbers, quickly:

- **Running watts (3,500):** what it produces continuously, all day. This is your budget.
- **Starting watts (~4,375):** a few seconds of surge for motor startups. This is your emergency fund.

And the rule I give every customer: **plan your steady load at no more than 80% of running watts — 2,800 watts on this size.** An engine loafing at 60–75% load runs cooler, quieter, and sips fuel. An engine pinned at 98% all night is wearing itself out. For the full sizing method, see [What Size Generator Do I Need?](/articles/what-size-generator-run-house).

## How many watts does everything in your house actually use?

Same table I use for every size, because the appliances don't care what generator you bought. Two numbers per item: running watts and the starting surge when a motor kicks on. Resistive loads (heaters, kettles, ovens) have no surge.

**Kitchen**

| Appliance | Running watts | Starting watts |
|---|---|---|
| Refrigerator (modern, 18–25 cu ft) | 150–250 | 800–1,200 |
| Chest freezer | 100–200 | 600–1,000 |
| Microwave | 1,000–1,200 | none |
| Coffee maker | 800–1,200 | none |
| Toaster oven | 1,200–1,500 | none |
| Electric kettle | 1,200–1,500 | none |

**Comfort and climate**

| Appliance | Running watts | Starting watts |
|---|---|---|
| Gas furnace blower (1/2 HP) | 600–900 | 1,500–2,200 |
| Window AC (8,000–10,000 BTU) | 900–1,200 | 2,200–3,000 |
| Window AC (12,000 BTU) | 1,200–1,500 | 3,000–3,600 |
| Portable space heater | 1,500 | none |
| Ceiling fan (each) | 50–100 | 150–250 |

**Water and essentials**

| Appliance | Running watts | Starting watts |
|---|---|---|
| Well pump (1/2 HP) | 1,000 | 2,000–3,000 |
| Well pump (1 HP) | 2,000 | 4,000–6,000 |
| Sump pump (1/3 HP) | 800 | 1,500–2,000 |
| Sump pump (1/2 HP) | 1,050 | 2,000–2,500 |
| Electric water heater (40–50 gal) | 4,500 | none |
| Electric dryer | 4,500–5,500 | none |

**Electronics and misc**

| Appliance | Running watts | Starting watts |
|---|---|---|
| LED lights (whole house) | 150–250 | none |
| Wi-Fi router + modem | 20–40 | none |
| TV + streaming box | 120–200 | none |
| Laptop / home office setup | 150–300 | none |
| Washing machine | 500–800 | 1,500–2,300 |
| Garage door opener | 500–700 | 1,000–1,400 |

Two items decide everything at this size. The window AC's 3,000-watt worst-case surge is 69% of your peak rating in a single event — it fits, but it's the event you plan around. And the electric water heater at 4,500 watts doesn't just strain this generator; it exceeds its running rating outright, alone, with nothing else plugged in.

## Combo 1: The essentials load (~2,100 watts)

The outage most people actually live through: food safe, basement dry, house warm.

| Load | Running watts |
|---|---|
| Refrigerator | 200 |
| Chest freezer | 150 |
| Sump pump (1/3 HP, cycling) | 800 (intermittent) |
| Gas furnace blower (winter) | 800 |
| 10 LED lights | 100 |
| Wi-Fi + modem | 30 |
| Phone and laptop charging | 70 |
| **Total steady load** | **~2,150** |

That's 61% of capacity. The worst moment — sump pump starting (2,000W surge) while the furnace blower runs and the fridge is on — peaks around 4,100 watts, inside the 4,375-watt ceiling but without much to spare. At this load the generator burns roughly 0.3–0.4 gal/hr and runs 10–12 hours on a typical tank. Sump sizing details live in [What Size Generator Do You Need for a Sump Pump?](/articles/generator-for-sump-pump).

## Combo 2: Essentials plus one window AC (~3,100 watts)

The summer version — the load that makes people say the outage "wasn't bad, actually," and the whole reason this class exists.

| Load | Running watts |
|---|---|
| Refrigerator | 200 |
| Chest freezer | 150 |
| Sump pump (1/3 HP, cycling) | 800 (intermittent) |
| Window AC (10,000 BTU, bedroom) | 1,000 |
| 15 LED lights | 150 |
| Wi-Fi, TV, chargers | 300 |
| Ceiling fans ×2 | 150 |
| **Total steady load** | **~2,750** |

Right at the 80% target, and the honest caveat is the startup choreography. The window AC's compressor starting (up to 3,000W) on top of a running sump pump is the trip scenario: in practice the two cycle on independent schedules and a direct collision is rare and brief — the peak rating absorbs a one-second overlap of the smaller events. What reliably trips people is adding the microwave (1,200W) while the AC runs. Drop the AC for the ten minutes you cook, or cook on the grill. Problem solved.

Winter version swaps the window AC for the furnace blower (800W) and lands around 2,500 watts, leaving room to rotate a 1,500-watt space heater in one closed room — in place of, not in addition to, something else big.

## Combo 3: The rotation load (~2,800 watts, managed)

Want the washing machine, the microwave, and the window AC across the same evening? You can — in sequence, not simultaneously:

- **Evening:** Combo 2 as listed — AC on, house cool, ~2,750W.
- **Dinner:** AC off for ten minutes, microwave on (1,200W) — peaks around 3,400W while food heats.
- **Laundry hour:** AC off, washer on (700W running, up to 2,300W surge as the agitator starts) — ~2,900W, and don't let the sump pump's start coincide; if the basement is dry, it's a non-issue in practice.

Nothing here exceeds the generator. It just requires the one skill this size demands: one big intermittent load at a time. After the first day, the rotation is habit. A middle step worth knowing is the [4,000-watt generator class](/articles/what-can-4000-watt-generator-run), which buys a few hundred watts of extra headroom for exactly these rotation moments without jumping all the way to 5,000 watts. If it sounds like a chore you don't want, that's the honest signal to buy the [5,000-watt class](/articles/what-can-5000w-generator-run), where Combo 2 and the microwave coexist.

## What can't a 3500 watt generator run?

Let me be blunt about the failures, because these are the returns and angry reviews:

- **Electric water heater:** 4,500 watts, alone, exceeds the 3,500-watt running rating. Not a rotation candidate, not a solo candidate. Gas water heating is what makes this size work; electric water heating is what breaks it.
- **Electric dryer:** 4,500–5,500 watts. Same answer, for the same reason. Line-dry for two days; you'll survive.
- **Central AC:** a 3-ton compressor needs 9,000–12,000 watts to start. The generator can't — voltage collapses, the compressor strains, and you're risking a four-figure compressor to save nothing. The full explanation is in [What Size Generator Do I Need to Run Central AC?](/articles/what-size-generator-central-ac).
- **1 HP well pump:** its 4,000–6,000W starting surge meets or beats the entire 4,375-watt peak. The 1/2 HP pump (2,000–3,000W surge) is this class's ceiling, and it fits.
- **Two window ACs plus a pump:** each piece fits alone; the second AC's startup landing on the sump pump's startup is the trip. One AC is this size's honest cooling answer.

Notice the pattern: everything on the "can't" list is a big electric *heating* load or a serious motor surge. Gas appliances are what make this size work.

## How much gas will it burn while doing all this?

At Combo 1, expect roughly 0.3–0.4 gal/hr; at Combo 2, closer to 0.45. A three-day outage at comfort load is roughly 25–35 gallons — plan cans and stabilizer accordingly. The inverter models sip less at light load because the engine throttles down; the Predator 3500's rated 11 hours at quarter load on 2.6 gallons works out to about 0.24 gal/hr. The per-size burn tables are in the broader [generator fuel consumption guide](/articles/generator-fuel-consumption).

## Which 3500 watt generators should you actually look at?

Two classes, two different ownership experiences:

- **Conventional open-frame (the workhorse):** the Champion and Westinghouse 3,500-running-watt open frames (~$350–550). Loud — low-to-mid 70s dB — mechanically simple, cheap to fix, and the best watts-per-dollar in the class. If it'll live in a shed 30 feet from the house, this is my default recommendation.
- **Inverter (the quiet one):** Predator 3500 (3,500 peak / 3,000 running, ~$800) — 57 dB at quarter load, electric start, 11-hour quarter-load runtime. It's the pick from our [quietest generators](/articles/quietest-generators-noise-levels) ranking, and the one to buy if the generator sits near a bedroom window or a neighbor's fence. Just remember to scale the combos to its 3,000 running watts.
- **Dual-fuel versions** of the open frames (~$450–650) trade about 10% of wattage on propane for fuel that stores forever and never gums a carburetor. If your load plan fits in ~3,150 watts, that's a trade worth making.

Skip any unit whose running watts are under 3,000 wearing a "3500" badge at a 3,500-watt price — you're buying Combo 1, not Combo 2.

## Who is a 3500 watt generator perfect for?

Small-to-medium homes where **heat, hot water, and cooking are gas**, and where one window AC is the summer requirement. Renters who can't do panel work and will run cords to the essentials plus the bedroom AC. Well-and-septic homes with a 1/2 HP pump. And campers stepping up from the 2,000-watt class who want a microwave and an AC in the RV without a second mortgage.

Size up if: your water heater or dryer is electric and non-negotiable, your well pump is 1 HP, or you want the microwave and the AC at the same time without thinking. Size down if it's purely fridge, lights, and phones — the [2,000-watt inverters](/articles/what-can-2000-watt-generator-run) do that quieter and cheaper.

One rule with no exceptions at any size: cords to appliances, or a proper transfer switch or interlock kit installed by someone licensed — never a plug into a dryer outlet, never a generator indoors or in a garage. Twenty feet from the house, outdoors, exhaust away.
