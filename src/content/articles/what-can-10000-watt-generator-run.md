**Quick answer:** A 10,000-watt generator runs a whole house — fridge, freezer, 1 HP well pump, furnace, lights, electronics — plus central AC up to 4 tons with a soft starter (5 tons with discipline), plus the electric water heater and dryer in light rotation. It still cannot run a tankless electric water heater, whole-house electric heat, or every big load in the same minute.

"What can a 10000 watt generator run?" is the question I get from homeowners who have done the smaller math, want central AC without an asterisk, and are standing at the fork between the biggest portables and a permanently installed standby. Here's the honest framing: 10,000 watts is the first portable class where the answer to almost every individual load is "yes" — including a 4-ton AC with a soft starter, the electric water heater, and the dryer. The limits that remain are about *combinations*, not items. The difference between happy owners and frustrated ones is still arithmetic, but at this size the arithmetic mostly forgives you. Let's do it anyway.

## Is that 10,000 watts running or starting?

Read the box carefully, because this is the class where peak-rating marketing is at its most aggressive. A true 10,000W generator delivers **10,000 running watts** and around **12,500 starting (peak) watts**. But the shelf is full of machines wearing "12500" or "13000" badges that run 10,000 — and machines wearing "10000" badges that are 10,000 *peak* and 8,000 running. Everything in this article is about the true 10,000-running-watt class. If the unit you're eyeing is 8,000 running, the [9,000-watt article](/articles/what-can-9000-watt-generator-run) is closer to your machine — scale accordingly.

The two numbers, quickly:

- **Running watts (10,000):** what it produces continuously, all day. This is your budget.
- **Starting watts (~12,500):** a few seconds of surge for motor startups. This is your emergency fund.

And the rule I give every customer: **plan your steady load at no more than 80% of running watts — 8,000 watts on this size.** At this capacity the 80% line isn't about protecting the engine from your house; it's about keeping surge headroom so a well pump start never coincides badly with an AC start. For the full sizing method, see [What Size Generator Do I Need?](/articles/what-size-generator-run-house).

## How many watts does everything in your house actually use?

Same table I use for every size, because the appliances don't care what generator you bought. Two numbers per item: running watts and the starting surge when a motor kicks on. Resistive loads (heaters, dryers, water heaters) have no surge.

**Kitchen**

| Appliance | Running watts | Starting watts |
|---|---|---|
| Refrigerator (modern, 18–25 cu ft) | 150–250 | 800–1,200 |
| Chest freezer | 100–200 | 600–1,000 |
| Microwave | 1,000–1,200 | none |
| Coffee maker | 800–1,200 | none |
| Toaster oven | 1,200–1,500 | none |
| Electric stove (one burner) | 1,200–2,500 | none |
| Dishwasher (heating cycle) | 1,200–1,800 | 1,500–2,000 |

**Comfort and climate**

| Appliance | Running watts | Starting watts |
|---|---|---|
| Central AC, 3-ton | 3,000–3,500 | 9,000–12,000 (3,500–4,500 with soft starter) |
| Central AC, 4-ton | 4,000–4,500 | 11,000–14,000 (4,500–5,500 with soft starter) |
| Central AC, 5-ton | 5,000–5,500 | 13,000–16,000 (5,500–7,000 with soft starter) |
| Gas furnace blower (1/2 HP) | 600–900 | 1,500–2,200 |
| Window AC (12,000 BTU) | 1,200–1,500 | 3,000–3,600 |
| Portable space heater | 1,500 | none |

**Water and essentials**

| Appliance | Running watts | Starting watts |
|---|---|---|
| Well pump (1/2 HP) | 1,000 | 2,000–3,000 |
| Well pump (1 HP) | 2,000 | 4,000–6,000 |
| Sump pump (1/2 HP) | 1,050 | 2,000–2,500 |
| Electric water heater (40–50 gal) | 4,500 | none |
| Electric dryer | 4,500–5,500 | none |

**Electronics and misc**

| Appliance | Running watts | Starting watts |
|---|---|---|
| LED lights (whole house) | 150–250 | none |
| Wi-Fi router + modem | 20–40 | none |
| TV + streaming box | 120–200 | none |
| Laptop / home office setup | 150–300 | none |
| Washing machine | 500–800 | 1,500–2,300 |
| Garage door opener | 500–700 | 1,000–1,400 |

Read the table with new eyes at this size: there is no single line item this generator can't run. The electric water heater is 45% of capacity. The dryer's worst case is 55%. Even the 5-ton AC's running draw fits. The only column that still bites is a *raw* (un-softened) big-AC surge: 13,000–16,000 watts for a 5-ton exceeds the 12,500-watt peak, which is why the soft starter stops being optional advice at this size and becomes part of the purchase.

## Combo 1: The essentials load (~2,400 watts)

The overnight baseline — food cold, basement dry, house warm, everything big asleep.

| Load | Running watts |
|---|---|
| Refrigerator | 200 |
| Chest freezer | 150 |
| Sump pump (1/2 HP, cycling) | 1,050 (intermittent) |
| Gas furnace blower (winter) | 800 |
| 10 LED lights | 100 |
| Wi-Fi + modem | 30 |
| Phone and laptop charging | 70 |
| **Total steady load** | **~2,400** |

Twenty-four percent of capacity. The generator loafs, and even loafing it burns more fuel than a 5,000-watt unit working at the same load — that's the standing tax of owning capacity, roughly 0.7–0.9 gal/hr at this light load for an open-frame conventional. If this combo is your whole plan, you bought two sizes too many; if it's your *overnight* plan inside a bigger day, it's exactly right.

## Combo 2: The everything day (~7,300 watts)

A normal day at home during an outage, all-electric house, laundry included. One big resistive load at a time is the only choreography left.

| Load | Running watts |
|---|---|
| Refrigerator + chest freezer | 350 |
| Well pump (1 HP, cycling) | 2,000 (intermittent) |
| Electric water heater (recovery cycles) | 4,500 (intermittent) |
| Washing machine | 700 (intermittent) |
| Microwave | 1,200 (intermittent) |
| 15 LED lights | 150 |
| Wi-Fi, TVs, chargers, home office | 550 |
| **Realistic steady load (one big load active)** | **~5,300–7,300** |

The swing factor is which intermittent load is on: water-heater recovery pushes the total toward 7,300; laundry or microwave time sits around 4,000–5,000. The dryer (5,000W) substitutes for the water heater's slot in the afternoon — the pair together (9,500W plus the fridge) is technically under the rating and practically over the planning line, so the rotation habit survives even here. The well pump's 6,000-watt surge lands on a 7,300-watt load for a worst-case 13,300 momentary — above the 12,500 peak on paper, which is why you don't run the water heater recovery during laundry: keep the coincident steady load at or under about 6,500 watts and every surge fits.

Fuel burn across this day: roughly 1.0–1.3 gal/hr averaged. For the full fuel math, see [How Much Fuel Does a Generator Use?](/articles/generator-fuel-consumption).

## Combo 3: Big-AC mode (~7,000 watts)

The combo that justifies the class. A 4-ton central AC with a soft starter fitted — the size that made the 9,000-watt class sweat — runs here with the house attached:

| Load | Running watts |
|---|---|
| Central AC, 4-ton (soft-started) | 4,500 |
| Refrigerator + chest freezer | 350 |
| Well pump (1 HP, cycling) | 2,000 (intermittent) |
| 15 LED lights | 150 |
| Wi-Fi, TVs, chargers, home office | 550 |
| **Total steady load** | **~7,550** |

Inside the 8,000-watt planning target. The soft starter keeps the compressor's start around 4,500–5,500 watts of surge — absorbed even with the pump mid-cycle. The water heater and dryer rotate in when the AC cycles off, which in most weather is several times an hour.

A 5-ton with a soft starter (running 5,000–5,500W) fits the same table with the pump as the only other allowed load and the resistive pair fully benched — possible, honest, and the practical ceiling of portable power. Past this, you're pricing standby units, and the [whole-house generator cost guide](/articles/whole-house-generator-cost) is the right next read. The soft-starter math itself is in [What Size Generator Do I Need to Run Central AC?](/articles/what-size-generator-central-ac).

## What can't a 10000 watt generator run?

The shortest "can't" list in this series — but it still has entries:

- **Tankless electric water heater:** 18,000–28,000W. Not close, not partial, not in rotation. No portable generator runs one; if tankless electric is your water heating, your outage plan is cold showers or a standby far up the kW range.
- **Whole-house electric heat:** a single 10kW furnace strip equals the entire generator; most electric furnaces run 15–20kW of strips. Electric-heat homes are standby homes.
- **Everything at once:** water heater (4,500) + dryer (5,500) + 4-ton AC (4,500) = 14,500 watts. The generator is big; it is not the grid. Two of the three, any two, is the standing rule.
- **A 5-ton AC without a soft starter:** 13,000–16,000W of surge against a 12,500W peak. It won't start, and repeated attempts are how compressors die.
- **An electric range at full tilt:** oven element plus all burners sums past 8,000–10,000W. One or two burners in rotation is fine forever.

## How much gas will it burn while doing all this?

At Combo 2 and 3 loads, roughly 1.0–1.3 gal/hr — call it 25–30 gallons for a three-day outage at whole-house comfort, which is why dual-fuel matters more at this size than any other: propane versions lose about 10% of wattage (9,000 running on propane) and gain fuel that stores indefinitely, runs cleaner, and comes in 40-lb tanks that last 10–12 hours each. The per-size burn tables are in the [generator fuel consumption guide](/articles/generator-fuel-consumption).

## Which 10000 watt generators should you actually look at?

Three classes, three different ownership experiences:

- **Conventional open-frame (the workhorse):** the DuroMax, Westinghouse, and Champion 10,000-class open frames (~$1,100–1,500). Loud — low-to-mid 70s dB — mechanically simple, electric start standard, and the cheapest honest route to whole-house portable power. At 200+ pounds with wheels, "portable" is a generous word; plan its parking spot before it arrives.
- **Dual-fuel versions:** (~$1,300–1,700) are the default recommendation at this size for the fuel reasons above. Size your combos against the ~9,000-watt propane number if propane is your primary fuel.
- **Large inverters:** (~$2,000–2,800) bring the noise down to the mid-60s dB, clean power for everything, and eco-mode sipping at the overnight load. If the generator pad sits near bedrooms or a close neighbor, this premium is the product. Our [quietest generators](/articles/quietest-generators-noise-levels) guide explains the dB math.

Whatever the class, verify the *running* watts on the spec sheet. In this class the gap between the carton number and the continuous number is where the returns come from.

## Who is a 10000 watt generator perfect for?

The medium-to-large home with a **4-ton central AC**, an **electric water heater**, a **dryer that matters**, and a **1 HP well pump** — the household that needed three asterisks at 7,500 watts and one at 9,000. Farms and workshops that also run a welder outlet or big power tools on the same machine. Anyone choosing deliberately against a standby because the house might be sold, the outages are annual, or the budget is real.

Go [9,000 watts](/articles/what-can-9000-watt-generator-run) if your AC is 3 tons and the rotation habit is fine. Go standby if outages are monthly, you're away from home for them, or the AC is 5 tons with an all-electric house behind it — the portable ceiling is honest, and this article just showed you where it is.
