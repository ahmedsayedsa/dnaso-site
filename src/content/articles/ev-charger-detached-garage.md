Installing an EV charger for a detached garage is the job nobody quotes you honestly over the phone. The charger itself is the cheap part — $400 to $700 for a good Level 2 unit. What you're actually paying for is getting 240 volts across your yard: a trench, conduit, fat copper wire, and usually a subpanel. I've run this job dozens of times, and the spread between a $1,500 install and a $4,000 install comes down to three things: distance, soil, and what's already in the garage. Here's the whole job, piece by piece, with real 2026 numbers.

## Why a Detached Garage EV Charger Costs More Than an Attached One

With an attached garage, the panel is usually on the other side of the wall. An electrician runs 30 feet of wire through a basement or attic, mounts the charger, and he's done by lunch — $600 to $1,200 all-in is typical.

A detached garage changes everything:

- **The run is underground.** That means trenching, conduit, and burial-depth rules.
- **The wire is bigger.** Distance causes voltage drop, and the fix is thicker (more expensive) copper.
- **You need a subpanel.** The NEC requires a disconnect and proper overcurrent protection in a detached structure fed by a feeder. You can't just land a 60A circuit on a junction box out there and call it done.
- **Permits and inspections** are essentially guaranteed, because it's new underground electrical work.

None of this is optional or gold-plating. It's code, and it's also just how you keep a 50-amp continuous load from becoming a problem in a building full of gasoline cans and lawn equipment.

## How Much Does Trenching for an EV Charger Cost?

The trench is the single biggest variable in the whole job. Here's what it actually costs in 2026:

| Method | Cost | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Hand digging (you or a laborer) | $5–$12 per foot hired out; free if it's your back | Fine for 30–50 ft in soft soil. Miserable in clay or rock. |
| Walk-behind trencher rental | ~$200/day (plus delivery) | Cuts a 4" wide trench 18–24" deep. A 100 ft run is a half-day job. |
| Contractor with a ride-on trencher | $8–$15 per foot, often with a $500–$800 minimum | Worth it for long runs or rocky ground. |
| Directional boring (under driveways/patios) | $15–$30 per foot for the bored section | Avoids tearing up concrete. Sometimes cheaper than demo + repour. |

A few things nobody tells you about the trench:

**Call 811 first.** It's free, it's the law in most states, and hitting a gas line with a trencher is the kind of day you don't forget. Locates take 2–3 business days. Plan around it.

**The trench is the cheap-looking part that eats weekends.** Homeowners rent a trencher for a "half day" and discover roots, a buried old sidewalk, and soil like concrete. If your run crosses a tree line, add time or money — one of the two.

**Backfill and lawn repair are on you** unless the quote says otherwise. A 100-foot trench across a nice lawn leaves a scar for a season. Ask about it before you sign, not after.

## Conduit, Burial Depth, and Wire: The Technical Core

This is where I see DIY attempts go wrong, so let's be specific.

**Conduit.** Use PVC Schedule 40 (Schedule 80 where it comes out of the ground and is exposed to damage — that's code, NEC 352.10 and the stub-up rule everyone forgets). For a 50–60A feeder, 1" conduit is the practical minimum; 1¼" makes the pull dramatically easier on long runs and costs almost nothing extra. Spend the extra $20. Future you, pulling wire through 100 feet of 1" PVC on a hot day, will thank present you.

**Burial depth (NEC Table 300.5), the numbers that matter:**

- PVC conduit under a yard: **18 inches** of cover minimum.
- Under a driveway: **24 inches**.
- Direct-burial UF cable (no conduit): **24 inches**, and honestly, don't. Conduit lets you replace or upsize wire later without re-digging your yard. UF is a false economy on a job like this.

**Wire sizing and voltage drop — the math.** An EV charger is a *continuous load*, so a "48A charger" needs a 60A circuit (48 ÷ 0.8), and a 40A charger goes on a 50A circuit. For the wire itself:

- **Up to ~60 ft:** 6 AWG copper THWN-2 handles a 60A feeder fine.
- **Around 100 ft:** Voltage drop at 48A continuous on 6 AWG runs about 3.5–4% — over the 3% guideline. Upsize to **4 AWG copper**. This is the run most detached garages actually have, and it's the upsell homeowners suspect is a scam. It isn't. Undersized wire on a long run means your charger sees low voltage for 8 hours straight, every night, for years.
- **150–200 ft:** You're into 3 AWG or 2 AWG copper, or considering aluminum feeders (2 AWG aluminum SER/URD-style runs are common and legal for feeders with the right terminations and anti-oxidant — and roughly half the cost of copper). Your electrician will price both.

You'll pull four conductors for a subpanel feed: two hots, a neutral, and a ground, plus a ground rod (usually two) at the garage itself. The neutral and ground stay **separate** in the subpanel — bonded only at the main panel. Getting that wrong is the most common subpanel mistake I get called to fix.

## Can You Skip the Trench and Use the Existing Circuit?

Sometimes, yes. This is the question worth asking before you spend anything, because roughly a third of the detached garages I look at already have usable power.

**It works when:**

- The garage has a dedicated 20A 120V circuit (or a lightly loaded one) and you drive **under ~35–40 miles a day**. A Level 1 charger on 120V adds 4–5 miles of range per hour. Parked 12 hours overnight, that's 50–60 miles back — plenty for a commuter with a short round trip. A Tesla or Hyundai/Kia usually includes or cheaply supports this; a portable Level 1 EVSE is $150–$250.
- The garage already has a 240V circuit from an old welder or compressor that's properly sized and not doing anything else. An electrician can verify the wire gauge and breaker in one visit ($100–$150) and land a NEMA 14-50 receptacle on it.

**It doesn't work when:**

- The garage is fed by a single old 15A circuit shared with lights, the door opener, and a freezer. A Level 1 charger pulls 12A continuously — that leaves almost nothing, and nuisance trips at 2 a.m. are how people end up extension-cording across the yard. (Never charge an EV on a regular extension cord, by the way. A 100 ft 16-gauge cord under a 12A continuous load is a space heater you buried in your lawn.)
- You drive 60+ miles a day or have a big-battery truck (Ford F-150 Lightning, Rivian) where Level 1 genuinely can't keep up.
- The existing feed is ancient knob-and-tube era wiring or an unpermitted handyman special. I find these constantly. If the garage wiring predates your house's last panel upgrade, assume it needs replacing.

Honest middle option: a **Level 2 charger dialed down to 24A or 32A** on a modest new run. Plenty of chargers (ChargePoint Home Flex, Emporia, Tesla Wall Connector) let you set the current. A 32A charger on a 40A circuit still adds ~25 miles per hour — five times Level 1 — and the smaller wire can shave real money off a long run. Whichever amperage you land on, you will still need to decide how the unit connects, and our [hardwired vs plug-in EV charger comparison](/articles/hardwired-vs-plug-in-ev-charger) explains why that choice affects amperage limits, GFCI behavior, and reliability.

## The Garage Subpanel: What It Costs and Why You Need It

For a new feeder, budget **$600–$1,200** for the subpanel portion: a 100A-rated panel (you're only feeding it 60A — the rating is just headroom), breakers, ground rods, and labor. Here's why it's not optional fluff:

- **Code requires a disconnect** in a detached structure, and a small panel is the clean way to do it.
- **You get room to grow.** Today's EV charger is 60A. Tomorrow you add lights that aren't embarrassing, a mini-split, a compressor. The panel's already there.
- **Load management gets easier.** If your main panel is tight, a load-management device (or a charger with dynamic load balancing, like the Tesla Wall Connector with a Neurio meter, or Emporia's Vue) can throttle the charger when the house is pulling hard — often saving you a $2,000+ main panel upgrade.

Speaking of which: if your house has a 100A main service that's already loaded, that's the hidden cost that blows up quotes. A service upgrade to 200A runs $2,000–$4,000 on top of everything. Ask about load management first — it's a $300–$600 solution to a $3,000 problem, and it's fully code-legal under NEC 220.57 / 625.42.

## Total Cost to Install an EV Charger in a Detached Garage

Adding it all up for a typical job — 60–100 ft run, 60A feeder, subpanel, mid-range charger:

| Line item | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| Level 2 charger (ChargePoint Home Flex, Emporia Pro, Tesla Wall Connector) | $400–$700 |
| Trenching (60–100 ft) | $500–$1,500 |
| Conduit + wire (upsized copper, 4 conductors) | $400–$900 |
| Garage subpanel + ground rods + breakers | $600–$1,200 |
| Permits + inspection | $100–$300 |
| Labor (panel work, terminations, charger install) | $500–$1,000 |
| **Total** | **$1,500–$4,000** |

Under $1,500 happens: short run, soft soil, you dig the trench yourself on a Saturday. Over $4,000 happens: 150+ ft, boring under a driveway, or a main service upgrade. Get two quotes, and make sure both are quoting the same wire size — a cheap quote on undersized 6 AWG over 120 feet is not a deal, it's a callback.

One more money note: the federal 30% tax credit for EV charging equipment (the Alternative Fuel Refueling Property credit) has been restricted to certain lower-income and rural census tracts in recent years, and the rules keep shifting. Check your address eligibility before you count on it — don't let a salesperson bake it into the quote as a sure thing.

## Permits for Trenching and Electrical Work: What to Expect

Two separate things get conflated here:

1. **The electrical permit** ($100–$300 typically) covers the feeder, subpanel, and charger. Expect one rough-in inspection (trench open, conduit in, before backfill — schedule before you fill, or you'll be re-digging for the inspector) and one final.
2. **The digging itself.** 811 locate is mandatory in practice everywhere. Some municipalities also want to know if you're crossing an easement or working in a right-of-way; if your trench stays on your own lot, it's rarely an issue.

Farm it out or DIY? In most jurisdictions a homeowner can pull their own permit for work on their own house. The trench and conduit are genuinely DIY-able if you're handy and patient. The panel terminations and subpanel are where I'd tell my own neighbor to hire it out — a loose lug on a 60A continuous load is a fire that takes its time. Most people land in the middle: homeowner digs (saving $500–$1,500), electrician does everything electrical. Just agree on trench depth and conduit size with your electrician *before* you dig, and take photos of the open trench with a tape measure in frame. Inspectors love that, and it can save you a re-dig.

## Mike's Bottom Line

Budget $1,500–$4,000, expect the trench and the wire to be most of it, and don't let anyone quote you without walking the route and measuring it. Upsize the conduit one step, upsize the wire when the run passes 75–100 feet, separate your neutrals and grounds in the subpanel, and photograph the open trench. Do those four things and this job is boring for the next 30 years — which is exactly what you want from electrical work.
