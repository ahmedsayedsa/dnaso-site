**Quick answer:** The quietest generator you can buy is the Honda EU2200i, rated at 48 dB(A) at quarter load and 57 dB(A) at full load, measured at 23 feet — quieter than normal conversation when it's loafing. The Westinghouse iGen2200 (52 dB) and Champion 2500 Dual Fuel (53 dB) are nearly as quiet for roughly half the price, and anything under 60 dB at 23 feet counts as genuinely quiet. A conventional open-frame portable, by contrast, runs 72–78 dB. Remember that load and distance move the real number: expect 5–9 dB more at full load, and about 6 dB less every time you double the distance.

If noise is the reason you're shopping, skip the marketing adjectives and look at one number: the manufacturer's dB rating at quarter load, measured at 23 feet (7 meters). Every genuinely quiet generator is an inverter model, and the best of them run at 48–60 dB — normal-conversation territory. The loud conventional portables they replace sit at 72–78 dB, which on a logarithmic scale is not "a bit louder." It's a different category of machine.

Here are the six quietest generators worth buying right now, ranked by verified noise rating, followed by the physics you need to read those ratings honestly — because load and distance move the real-world number more than brand does.

**How to read these numbers:** all six are inverter generators, and all six ratings follow the industry convention — dB(A) at 23 feet under 25% load. That flatters every machine. Expect roughly 5–9 dB more at full load, and more again if you stand closer than 23 feet. The ranking still holds, because every model was measured the same way — but a "48 dB" generator is only 48 dB while it's loafing.

## The picks, model by model

### Honda EU2200i — the quietest generator you can buy

Honda's own spec sheet rates the EU2200i at **48 dB(A) at quarter load, 57 dB(A) at rated load** — the lowest published figures in the class, and independent testing consistently keeps it at or near the top. Output is 2,200 peak / 1,800 running watts from the 121cc GXR120 engine, with 8.1 hours of runtime at quarter load on a 0.95-gallon tank. You pay for it: roughly double the price of the Westinghouse below, for the same wattage. What the money buys is the noise floor, the build quality, a 3-year residential warranty, and the strongest dealer network in the business. If it will run overnight near bedrooms for a decade, this is the one.

### Westinghouse iGen2200 — nearly as quiet, half the price

Westinghouse rates the iGen2200 at **52 dBA** — just 4 dB behind the Honda — with the same 2,200 peak / 1,800 running watts, up to 12 hours at quarter load on its 1.14-gallon tank, and a weight of about 46 lb. The double-insulated enclosure and low-tone muffler do the quiet work. It's parallel-capable, carries a 3-year limited warranty, and typically sells for $450–550. The current iGen2200c variant adds a CO sensor. The honest caveat: Westinghouse's support network and long-term parts availability aren't Honda's. As a value pick for camping, tailgating, and light home backup, nothing else is this quiet at this price.

### Champion 2500 Dual Fuel — the quietest way to run propane

Champion rates model 200962 at **53 dBA from 23 feet**, and it's the only pick here that runs on gasoline or propane out of the box: 2,500 starting watts either way, 1,850 running watts on gasoline, 1,665 on propane, with up to 11.5 hours on a gallon of gas or around 34 hours on a 20 lb propane tank at quarter load. At 39 lb it's also the lightest generator in this table — genuinely one-hand portable. Propane stores indefinitely and burns cleaner, which makes this the natural pick for apartment and balcony-adjacent backup, RV use, and anyone who hates stale gasoline. (If you're weighing fuels more broadly, our [generator for an apartment](/articles/generator-for-an-apartment) guide covers the storage rules that come with them.)

### Yamaha EF2200iS — the runtime pick

Yamaha's spec for the EF2200iS is **57 dB at quarter load** (rising toward 65 dB at full load), so it's not the quietest here — but it answers with the biggest tank (1.24 gallons) and the longest quarter-load runtime in the 2,200W class: **10.5 hours**. Output is 2,200 peak / 1,800 running watts, and uniquely in this group it includes a 30A RV outlet, making it properly RV-ready without adapters. Yamaha's Smart Throttle does the same load-matching trick as Honda's Eco-Throttle. Build quality is excellent and the warranty is 3 years. Choose it over the Honda when runtime and the RV outlet matter more than the last few decibels.

### Predator 3500 — quiet, with real wattage

The Predator 3500 is the step up when 1,800 running watts isn't enough. Harbor Freight rates it at **57 dB at 25% load** (its owner's manual specifies 56 dB(A) at 23 feet), while delivering 3,500 peak / 3,000 running watts — enough for a fridge, lights, a microwave, and a small window AC together, which no other pick here can claim. Electric start, an 11-hour quarter-load runtime from the 2.6-gallon tank, and a street price around $800 make it the watt-per-dollar leader. The compromises: it weighs about 100 lb, and Harbor Freight's 2-year warranty and service network are thinner than Honda's or Yamaha's.

### Generac GP2500i — the loudest pick, still quieter than most

At **60 dB at 25% load**, the GP2500i is the loudest generator on this list — and still a full 12+ dB below a conventional portable. It earns its place with the strongest output in the small class (2,500 peak / 2,200 running watts), Generac's COsense carbon-monoxide shutoff, and an 8-hour quarter-load runtime. It's a sensible middle option: more muscle than the 2,200W crowd, from the biggest name in home standby power, at a mid-pack price. Just don't buy it specifically for silence — the Honda, Westinghouse, and Champion are all meaningfully quieter for similar money.

## Why inverter generators are so much quieter

Three engineering differences stack up. First, **engine speed**: a conventional generator runs at a fixed 3,600 RPM no matter the load, while an inverter throttles down — sometimes to ~2,000 RPM — when demand is light, and engine noise tracks RPM closely. Second, **the enclosure**: inverter portables are wrapped in insulated housings; open-frame conventionals radiate noise in every direction. An enclosure alone is worth roughly 5–10 dB. Third, **exhaust tuning**: inverter units carry larger, better-matched mufflers, and lower RPM softens each exhaust pulse. The trade-off is cost per watt and a ceiling around 7,500W in portable inverter sizes. ## The decibel scale, in 30 seconds

Decibels are logarithmic, not linear — and that one fact explains most generator-noise confusion:

- **+10 dB = 10× the sound intensity**, and it feels roughly twice as loud to human ears.
- **+3 dB ≈ double the intensity** — a small gap on paper, clearly audible in person.
- The whole generator conversation happens between 48 and 78 dB — a 30 dB spread that represents a **1,000× intensity range**.

For reference: a quiet bedroom at night is ~30 dB, normal conversation at 3 feet is 60–65 dB, a vacuum cleaner at 10 feet is ~70 dB, and a gas lawn mower at the operator's ear is 85–90 dB. When the Honda at 48 dB and a conventional portable at 72 dB sit 24 dB apart, that's roughly 250× the sound intensity. "Slightly louder" it is not.

## Distance: the free noise reduction

Sound drops about **6 dB every time you double the distance** from the source. That makes placement the cheapest noise control you own:

| Distance from generator | Change vs. 23-ft rating | A 60 dB unit reads as |
|---|---|---|
| 12 ft | +6 dB | ~66 dB |
| 23 ft (rated distance) | 0 | 60 dB |
| 46 ft | −6 dB | ~54 dB |
| 92 ft | −12 dB | ~48 dB |
| 184 ft | −18 dB | ~42 dB |

Two limits on this trick. One is safety: carbon monoxide rules come first, so the generator stays **at least 20 feet from the house** with the exhaust pointed away — our placement guide on [how far a generator should be from the house](/articles/how-far-should-generator-be-from-house) has the full reasoning and cord-sizing math (voltage drop is real; use 10-gauge cord for runs over 50 feet at 30 amps). The other is physics: the 6 dB rule assumes open space. Between houses, reflections off walls can add 2–3 dB back. Measure at your property line with a phone dB app instead of trusting the arithmetic.

**Worked example:** say the loudest pick here, the 60 dB Generac GP2500i, sits 23 feet from your neighbor's bedroom window. At that distance it reads its rated 60 dB — conversation level, clearly audible through a closed window. Move it to 46 feet and the same machine reads about 54 dB; at 92 feet, about 48 dB — the Honda's quarter-load rating, achieved with a tape measure instead of a purchase. Now run the Honda the other way: its 48 dB at 23 feet becomes roughly 42 dB at 46 feet and 36 dB at 92 feet, close to a quiet bedroom's 30 dB. Then add the load correction: at full load, add 5–9 dB back to any of these figures, which is why a generator that seems silent while charging phones can still annoy the street while running a microwave and a window AC together.

## Campground, HOA, and ordinance rules that actually bite

- **Campgrounds:** most enforce quiet hours — commonly 10 p.m. to 6 or 7 a.m. — when generators may not run at all, whatever their rating. US National Park Service campgrounds additionally cap generator noise at **60 dB measured at 50 feet**. Every pick above passes that cap at moderate load; a conventional portable does not.
- **Local ordinances:** residential limits typically sit at 55–65 dB at the property line, with stricter nighttime numbers. A 72 dB conventional at 23 feet violates most of them; a 48–53 dB inverter set back in your lot usually complies.
- **HOAs:** often stricter than the city — some ban portables outright, limit runtime hours, or require standby units to be screened. Check your CC&Rs before you buy, not after.
- **Neighbor strategy:** warn them before the storm, point the exhaust away from their windows, offer an extension cord for their fridge and phones, and respect quiet hours by cycling the fridge before bed. Goodwill is a noise-control technology too.

## Already own a loud one? Quieter is possible

Distance first (the 6 dB rule above is free), then: set the unit on grass or dirt instead of concrete, which transmits vibration; point the exhaust away from houses and reflective walls; use a purpose-built, ventilated generator enclosure for 5–10 dB — never anything airtight, since CO buildup and overheating are lethal and destructive respectively; and consider an aftermarket muffler for 2–5 dB, checking it doesn't choke exhaust flow. What doesn't work: blankets (fire and CO hazard) and "the garage with the door cracked" (CO reaches lethal levels in minutes — this kills people every year).
