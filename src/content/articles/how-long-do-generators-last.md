The honest answer: a standby generator's life is measured in **hours, not years** — and for a typical homeowner, the hour rating translates to decades. An air-cooled standby rated for 2,500 hours will usually outlast the roof over it. The units that die young almost always die from neglect, not from running out of hours.

## Lifespan by generator type

| Type | Realistic engine life | What that means in practice |
|---|---|---|
| Air-cooled standby (Generac Guardian, Kohler, Cummins, Champion) | 2,000–3,000 hours | 20–30 years of typical outage + exercise use |
| Liquid-cooled standby (36kW+) | 10,000+ hours | Effectively indefinite for residential use; the engine outlives the electronics |
| Portable (gasoline) | 1,000–2,000 hours | 10–20 years of occasional outage use |
| Inverter portable (Honda EU, Westinghouse iGen, Champion inverter) | 2,000+ hours | Longer than conventional portables; quieter and more fuel-efficient too |

Air-cooled standby engines are V-twins running at 3,600 RPM — essentially beefed-up lawn-tractor engines. They're not designed for continuous duty the way liquid-cooled 1,800 RPM engines are. That 2,000–3,000 hour rating is the honest number; anyone promising more from an air-cooled unit is selling.

## What "hours" actually mean in real outage years

This is where the math gets reassuring. A standby generator's hour meter counts three things: outages, the weekly exercise cycle, and any manual run time. Here's a typical year:

- **Weekly exercise:** 5–12 minutes per week ≈ **5–11 hours per year**
- **Typical outages:** most US homes see roughly **40–100 outage hours per year** (heavily region-dependent — Gulf Coast and wildfire country see more, the upper Midwest less)

Call it ~100 hours a year all-in. A 2,500-hour engine at 100 hours per year = **25 years**. Even in a storm-prone area at 200 hours per year, you're looking at 12+ years of engine life — and the engine is rarely what fails first. Controllers, batteries, and transfer switch components usually need attention long before the engine wears out.

The takeaway: don't shop standby generators by lifespan anxiety. Shop by sizing, installer quality, and maintenance — those decide the real outcome.

## The exercise cycle: why those 12 minutes matter

Every modern standby runs a weekly self-test — typically 5–12 minutes at low speed, no load. It seems trivial. It does four things that keep the unit alive:

1. **Circulates oil** so seals and bearings don't sit dry for months.
2. **Charges the battery** from the onboard charger (weak batteries are the #1 no-start cause — more below).
3. **Burns off moisture** that condenses in the crankcase and exhaust between runs.
4. **Runs diagnostics** — low oil, overcrank, sensor faults — so you find out about a problem on a quiet Tuesday instead of during a hurricane.

If your unit has a "quiet test" mode, use it — it exercises at lower RPM and keeps the neighbors happy. But don't disable the exercise cycle to save a few cents of fuel. The units that fail when needed are disproportionately the ones whose exercise was turned off or whose fault codes were ignored for months.

## What kills generators early

**Neglected oil.** The #1 engine killer. Air-cooled engines run hot and shear oil down fast. Most manufacturers call for an oil change every 100–200 running hours or **once a year, whichever comes first** — and "whichever comes first" means yearly for almost every homeowner. Old oil sludges, bearings starve, and a $60 oil change becomes a $3,000 engine.

**Stale fuel (portables).** Gasoline goes stale in 3–6 months, gums up carburetors, and is the classic reason a portable won't start when needed. This is one of the quiet advantages of standby units: natural gas and propane don't go stale, so fuel is never the failure point. For portables: use fuel stabilizer, run the carb dry after use, or switch to propane.

**Battery death.** The battery is the single most common no-start cause on standby units. Heat, age, and trickle-charger failures kill them in **2–4 years** typically. Replace proactively every 2–3 years — a $80–$150 battery is cheap insurance against a dead unit in a storm. If your controller throws low-battery warnings, don't snooze them.

**Carbon buildup from light-load running.** Air-cooled engines that run for hours at very light load (just a fridge and a few lights) build carbon deposits on valves and plugs — "wet stacking" in diesel terms, carbon fouling in gas terms. The weekly exercise plus real outage loads usually prevent this, but a unit that only ever idles through its exercise cycle benefits from an occasional loaded run.

**Coolant neglect (liquid-cooled).** Liquid-cooled units need coolant changes and thermostat/hose inspection on schedule, just like a car. Neglected coolant leads to overheating, and overheating kills engines fast.

**Rodents and nests.** Mice love generator enclosures — warm, sheltered, full of chewable wires. Inspect the enclosure a few times a year, especially in fall. Chewed control wiring causes phantom fault codes and no-starts.

<div class="safety"><strong>Safety note:</strong> Portable generators produce carbon monoxide that can kill in minutes. Never run one indoors, in a garage (even with the door open), or near windows, doors, or vents — keep it at least 20 feet from the house with the exhaust pointed away. Standby units are permanently installed outdoors to engineered clearances for the same reason.</div>

## Maintenance habits that add years

The full routine is in our [generator maintenance checklist](/articles/generator-maintenance-checklist). The short version that covers 90% of it:

- **Oil and filter:** yearly or per the manual's hour interval — whichever comes first. Use the specified weight (typically 5W-30 synthetic for air-cooled).
- **Air filter and spark plugs:** inspect yearly, replace per schedule (plugs typically every 200–400 hours or 2 years).
- **Battery:** replace every 2–3 years, proactively. Keep terminals clean.
- **Exercise cycle:** leave it on, check monthly that it actually ran (most controllers log it; many units now have phone apps).
- **Annual professional service:** $200–$400 for a dealer service visit — oil, filters, plugs, battery test, transfer switch inspection, load test. Worth it, especially while under warranty (some warranties require documented maintenance).
- **Keep the enclosure clear:** 18 inches minimum around the unit, no mulch piled against it, no shrubs growing into the vents. Overheating from blocked airflow is a slow killer.

Do this and the realistic outcome is 20–30 years from an air-cooled standby. Skip it and you can kill a $6,000 unit in under five.

## Signs your generator is aging out

Hours and age tell part of the story; behavior tells the rest. Start planning for replacement when you see these stack up:

- **Rising oil consumption** between changes — worn rings or valve seals, and it only gets worse.
- **Hard starting or frequent overcrank faults**, especially after the battery and starter test fine.
- **Fault codes that keep coming back** after the underlying part is replaced — often a failing controller board.
- **Visible corrosion** on the enclosure base, alternator housing, or transfer switch contacts. Salt air and road salt shorten enclosure life well below engine life.
- **Parts on backorder or discontinued.** Once the manufacturer stops supporting the controller, one failed board totals the unit.

One or two of these on a 10-year-old unit is a repair. Four of them on an 18-year-old unit is a replacement wearing a repair's clothes.

## When to replace vs. repair

Use the **50% rule**: if a repair costs more than half of a new installed unit, replace. A $3,500 engine rebuild on a 15-year-old 14kW doesn't make sense when a new unit installed is $9,000 with a fresh 5-year warranty.

Replace rather than repair when:

- The unit is 15+ years old **and** needs a major repair (engine, controller, or transfer switch)
- Parts are discontinued or hard to find (common past the 15–20 year mark)
- Repair costs exceed 50% of new installed price
- Your power needs have grown (added AC, EV charger, home addition) beyond the unit's capacity

Repair when:

- The unit is under 10 years old and the failure is a component (battery charger, starter, sensor, battery)
- It's a transfer switch issue — the switch can often be serviced or replaced independently of the generator
- The engine is healthy (good compression, clean oil history) and the problem is electrical

One nuance: a well-maintained 20-year-old air-cooled unit with low hours can still have plenty of life left. Age alone isn't the trigger — it's age **plus** a major failure **plus** parts availability. Check the hour meter before you decide; a 20-year-old unit with 400 hours on it is barely broken in.

## Bottom line

Expect **2,000–3,000 hours (20–30 years of normal use)** from an air-cooled standby, **10,000+ hours** from liquid-cooled, and **1,000–2,000 hours** from a portable. The hour rating is generous for residential use — what actually decides lifespan is oil changes, batteries, and whether the exercise cycle runs. Maintain it and it'll be there when the lights go out; neglect it and it won't.
