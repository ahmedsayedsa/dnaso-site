The first generator decision isn't really portable vs standby. It's inverter vs regular (conventional) — because that one choice sets your noise level, your power quality, and your price per watt before anything else does. A regular portable is cheap watts and loud ones. An inverter is quiet, clean power that costs roughly double per watt. Only after you've settled that does the third option — a standby unit that starts itself — enter the picture.

This guide leads with the inverter-vs-regular contrast, then brings standby in, so you can see all three types side by side, with real numbers.

## What Each Type Actually Is

### Portable generator

A portable generator is a gasoline-powered engine bolted to a steel frame, usually with wheels and a handle. You wheel it outside, start it (pull cord or electric start), and plug things into the outlets on its panel — either extension cords run to your fridge and lamps, or, better, a manual transfer switch or interlock wired into your panel.

This is the classic blackout machine. Champion, Westinghouse, Predator (Harbor Freight), and Honda's EU series all live here. A mid-size portable in the 7,500–9,500 watt range runs about $700–$1,500 for conventional units.

**What it does well:** It's cheap, it's flexible, and you can lend it to a neighbor. You can run essentials — fridge, freezer, some lights, a window AC or small sump pump — off a 7,500W unit without much trouble. You can also throw it in the truck for a jobsite or tailgate.

**What it doesn't do:** It never starts by itself. If the outage hits while you're at work, your fridge is warm until you get home. Conventional portable units are loud — 72–78 dB at 25 feet, roughly a vacuum cleaner running all night. And the power quality from conventional portables is rough, which matters for electronics (more on that below).

### Standby generator

A standby generator is a permanent installation. It sits on a concrete pad beside your house like an oversized AC condenser, wired directly into your electrical panel through an automatic transfer switch (ATS). When utility power drops, the ATS detects it, and the generator starts — typically within 10–30 seconds. When power returns, it switches back and shuts itself down. You do nothing. Most run on natural gas or propane.

Generac and Kohler dominate this market. A 22–26 kW air-cooled standby unit costs roughly $4,500–$7,000 for the equipment; installed, most homeowners land between $10,000 and $15,000 once you count the transfer switch, gas line, pad, permits, and electrician.

**What it does well:** True hands-off backup. Sized right, it runs your whole house — central AC, well pump, electric range. It also adds resale value and, in outage-prone areas, some insurers care about it.

**What it doesn't do:** It's a major purchase and a permanent fixture. Installation takes weeks, not an afternoon. It also needs scheduled maintenance (see the [generator maintenance checklist](/articles/generator-maintenance-checklist/)) — these machines exercise themselves weekly, which helps, but they still need annual service. And you're now dependent on your gas supply: natural gas is nearly always flowing, but propane means monitoring a tank.

### Inverter generator

An inverter generator is technically a portable, but with a different power-production design. A conventional portable spins an alternator at fixed speed and the raw output varies in quality. An inverter generator produces AC, converts it to DC, then inverts it back to clean AC — the electronics throttle the engine to match the load.

The practical results: they're quieter (48–62 dB, about conversation level), they sip fuel (many can run 8–10 hours on a couple gallons at light load), and they produce clean power with total harmonic distortion (THD) under 3%.

Honda EU2200i ($1,100–$1,300) is the benchmark; Predator 3500 ($900–$1,000), Champion 4500W, and Westinghouse iGen4500 are strong budget alternatives in the $700–$1,100 range.

**What it does well:** Quiet enough for a campground or a neighborhood without complaints. Safe for anything with a circuit board. Efficient.

**What it doesn't do:** Wattage per dollar is poor. A $1,000 inverter gets you 3,500W; a $1,000 conventional portable gets you 9,500W. Two or more inverter units can be paralleled with a kit to add capacity, but costs climb fast.

### The parallel-kit option, spelled out

A parallel kit ($60–$100 from Champion, Westinghouse, or Predator) lets you link two compatible inverter generators so their outputs combine. Two Honda EU2200i units give you about 4,400W of starting power for roughly $2,400–$2,600 total — versus one 4,500W inverter at $900–$1,100. Why would anyone do that? Redundancy and flexibility: run one unit alone for light loads (quieter, sips fuel), fire up the second only when the well pump or microwave needs to join. It's also easier to lift two 47-lb units than one 100-lb machine. The math rarely wins on price alone, but for RV owners who already own one small inverter, buying a second beats buying a whole bigger machine.

## The Clean-Power Issue: Why THD Matters

Total harmonic distortion is a measure of how "clean" the AC power is. Utility power is under 5%. Your electronics — laptops, TVs, routers, smart thermostats, modern furnace controls — expect that.

Conventional portable generators often run 10–25% THD. For resistive loads (toasters, incandescent lights) and simple motors, that doesn't matter. For electronics, dirty power can cause glitches, shorten component life, or in worst cases damage power supplies. This is the single biggest reason electricians steer homeowners toward inverter units or standby units for anything beyond the basics.

Inverter generators and most standby units hold THD under 3–5% — essentially utility-grade power. If you're backing up a home office, a modern HVAC system, or anything with a control board, clean power isn't a luxury; it's a requirement. And one note: a whole-house surge protector at the panel is cheap insurance alongside any generator — a lightning strike doesn't care about your backup plan.

<div class="safety"><strong>Safety note:</strong> Never run any generator indoors or in a garage — carbon monoxide kills fast and silently. Keep portables at least 20 feet from the house, exhaust pointed away. A $25 battery CO detector near sleeping areas is cheap insurance.</div>

## Who Each Type Is For

**Buy a portable if:** outages are occasional (a few per year, hours not days), your budget is under $2,000, and you're okay doing the work — wheeling it out, filling it with gas, running cords. Best for renters, smaller homes, and "keep the fridge cold and the phone charged" scenarios. With a proper interlock kit and inlet box, a 7,500W portable can keep a lot of a house alive.

**Buy a standby if:** outages are frequent or long in your area, you have medical equipment, a well pump, or a sump pump that can't wait, you travel often, or you simply never want to think about it. This is the answer for rural homes on well water and anyone with a basement that floods without a pump.

**Buy an inverter if:** noise matters (close neighbors, HOA, campgrounds), you're powering electronics-heavy loads, or you want the same machine to serve your house and your RV/camping. A 4,500W inverter generator is the sweet spot for most homeowners who want quiet backup of essentials.

**Consider two inverters in parallel if:** you want quiet, clean power but 2,000W isn't enough. A pair of 2,200W units with a parallel kit gives you ~4,400W starting power for roughly the cost of one larger inverter — and you can run just one for light loads.

## Hidden Costs Nobody Mentions Up Front

The sticker price is never the whole price. Budget for these:

- **Portable + safe home connection:** a manual transfer switch or interlock kit installed by an electrician runs $500–$1,500. Extension cords alone are free, but running cords through windows in a storm gets old fast — and it's a security and CO risk.
- **Standby installation extras:** beyond the $10,000–$15,000 typical total — concrete pad ($200–$500), gas line extension if the meter is far ($500–$2,000), permits ($100–$500 depending on jurisdiction), and possibly a larger gas meter from the utility ($300–$800). Get three quotes; standby install pricing varies wildly by region.
- **Inverter:** usually just the unit and a heavy-duty extension cord. The cheapest total cost of ownership to get running.

Factor in fuel, too: a portable running a three-day outage burns $200–$275 in gasoline (see [dual-fuel vs gasoline vs propane](/articles/dual-fuel-vs-gas-vs-propane-generator/) for the math). Standby owners on natural gas pay $50–$100 for the same outage. Over a decade of storm seasons, that gap adds up.

## The Honest Downsides

- **Portable:** Loud. Manual everything. Gasoline goes stale (3–6 months without stabilizer). Theft is real — cable-lock it. You *must* be home when the power fails.
- **Standby:** $10,000–$15,000 installed is the real price for most homes. You're locked to your fuel source. Maintenance isn't optional — skip the annual service and the weekly exercise and it'll fail when you need it. Some HOAs restrict placement and noise.
- **Inverter:** Costs roughly double per watt compared to conventional portables. Larger models (5,000W+) get heavy. Most still need you to be home to start them.

## The Decision Framework

Run through these in order:

1. **Do you want it to start automatically?** Yes → standby. No, I'm fine doing it myself → keep going.
2. **How long are your typical outages?** Hours → portable or inverter. Days → standby, or a big portable plus a serious fuel plan.
3. **What's your budget, all-in?** Under $2,000 → portable or inverter. $10,000+ → standby.
4. **What are you powering?** Electronics-heavy or noise-sensitive → inverter. Whole house including central AC or well pump → standby (or the biggest portable you can manage). Simple essentials → conventional portable.
