A sump pump is the one load in your house that cannot wait until morning. When the power dies in the middle of a storm — which is exactly when outages happen — groundwater doesn't pause. The pit fills, the float rises, and if nothing lifts that water out, your basement takes it instead. I've stood in enough flooded basements to know the math is brutal: a pump moving 40 gallons a minute is the only thing between a wet floor and a five-figure cleanup.

So the sizing question isn't academic. Get it wrong and the generator you own can't start the pump you depend on. This guide works through the real numbers — running watts, starting watts, and the generator size each common pump actually needs — plus the battery and water-powered alternatives worth considering.

## Why does a sump pump need so much power to start?

Because it's an induction motor, and motors are liars. The wattage on the label describes the pump cruising. Starting is a different event entirely.

When a motor first energizes, the rotor isn't spinning yet, so there's no back-EMF resisting current flow. For a fraction of a second the motor gulps two to three times its running current while it hauls itself up to speed against a column of water. Electricians call this locked-rotor or inrush current; you'll see it as the "starting watts" or "surge watts" figure.

That surge is why a generator that looks big enough on paper fails in practice. Your pump might cruise at 1,050 watts, but if the generator can't deliver roughly 2,150 watts for that first half-second, the motor just hums, heats, and never spins — and the generator's overload protection trips to save it. Size for the surge, run for the average.

## How many watts does each sump pump size use?

These are the standard figures for the three residential sizes you'll find in nearly every basement pit in America:

| Pump size | Running watts | Starting (surge) watts | Minimum generator (pump alone) | Sensible generator (pump + fridge + lights) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1/3 HP | ~800W | ~1,300W | 2,000W | 3,500W |
| 1/2 HP | ~1,050W | ~2,150W | 3,000W | 3,500–4,500W |
| 3/4 HP | ~1,500W | ~3,000W | 4,000W | 5,000W+ |

A few notes before you take the table shopping:

- **Check your pump's amp rating and multiply by 120.** A pump labeled 9.7 amps draws about 1,160 running watts — close enough to the table, but your actual pump wins any argument with a generic chart. The nameplate is on the pump or in its manual.
- **Aging pumps surge harder.** Bearings drag, capacitors weaken, and a ten-year-old pump can pull noticeably more on startup than it did new. If your pump is old and your generator is marginal, that's the combination that fails at 2 a.m.
- **The check valve matters too.** A failed check valve lets the water column fall back into the pit, so the pump restarts more often and works against a heavier column. More starts, more surges, more fuel.

## Which generator size should you actually buy?

If you don't own one yet, here's how I'd steer you:

**2,000-watt inverter — only for a 1/3 HP pump, alone.** It starts the small pump and sips fuel doing it, which matters in a long outage. But there's no headroom for a fridge, and if you ever replace the pump with a 1/2 HP, you're shopping again.

**3,500 watts — the sweet spot for most homes.** Starts a 1/2 HP pump with the fridge already running, handles lights, a furnace blower, and device charging, and it's still a one-person carry. This is the size I recommend when a customer says "sump pump plus the basics."

**5,000 watts and up — for 3/4 HP pumps and whole-basement confidence.** A 3/4 HP pump's 3,000-watt surge eats most of a small generator's capacity by itself. At 5,000 watts you stop doing math during storms. Fuel consumption climbs, though — a bigger engine drinks more at the same load, which is worth understanding before a three-day outage. ## Can a power station run a sump pump instead?

A big lithium power station is tempting: silent, no fuel, safe indoors, and it can sit next to the pit ready to go. Two honest limitations:

**Surge first.** The station's surge rating must clear your pump's starting watts — not its running watts. Many popular 1 kWh stations top out around 1,800–2,000 watts of surge, which stalls a 1/2 HP pump dead. The newer 2 kWh-class units with 2,400W+ continuous output and 4,000W+ surge handle a 1/2 HP fine.

**Runtime second.** A cycling pump in heavy rain might average 150–300 watts over an hour, but the station only holds what it holds. A 2 kWh station gives you roughly 6–10 hours of storm-duty pumping. That's a bridge, not a solution — long enough for a passing thunderstorm, not for a two-day nor'easter. The [portable power stations guide](/articles/portable-power-stations-home-backup) goes deeper on realistic station runtimes, and the [battery versus generator comparison](/articles/home-battery-vs-generator) lays out where each one wins.

Where a station genuinely shines is as the *automatic* layer: plugged in and charged, it catches the first hours of an outage before you're home to start a generator.

## What about a dedicated battery backup sump pump?

This is a different animal from a power station: a second, smaller 12-volt DC pump sitting in the same pit, powered by a deep-cycle battery on a maintainer charger. Your main pump does the daily work; the backup only wakes when the power's out or the main pump fails.

The case for one is strong:

- **It's automatic.** No generator to drag out, no cords, no being home. The float switch handles everything.
- **It covers pump failure, not just power failure.** A generator does nothing for you when the main pump itself dies on a rainy Tuesday while you're at work. A backup pump does.
- **Runtime is measured in gallons, not hours.** Because it cycles, a healthy 100 Ah battery moves several thousand gallons across a storm.

The case against: batteries age quietly. A backup system with a five-year-old battery is a decoration. Budget for a replacement battery every 3–5 years and test the system twice a year — pour a bucket of water in the pit and watch it work. If you want the layered approach (backup pump for the first hours, generator for the long haul), you've built the setup I'd build in my own house.

## Are water-powered backup pumps worth it?

If you're on municipal water, there's a third option with no battery and no fuel at all: a water-powered backup pump. It uses your home's water pressure as the energy source — roughly one gallon of city water drives about two gallons of pit water out (the ratio varies by model and pressure).

**Worth it if:** you're on city water with solid pressure (these need a minimum, typically around 20 psi at the device, and work better well above it), your outages are usually short, and you want a zero-maintenance backup that never needs a battery swap.

**Not worth it if:** you're on a well — when the power dies, your well pump dies, and the water-powered pump has nothing to work with. Also skip it if your water bill is painful or your municipality restricts them; a long storm can run a lot of city water through the ejector.

## How long will the pump run your generator during a storm?

People picture the pump running constantly. In reality it cycles: pit fills, float rises, pump empties the pit in 10–30 seconds, silence for a minute or five. Your generator's fuel burn follows the *average* load, and the pump's average contribution is modest — often under 200 watts across an hour even in steady rain.

The exception is a true deluge, when inflow nearly matches the pump's capacity and it runs almost continuously. That's when your fuel plan gets tested. A 3,500-watt conventional generator at light load burns roughly 0.3–0.45 gallons an hour; across a 24-hour storm day that's 7–11 gallons. Two five-gallon cans in the garage is a plan. One is a hope. ## How do you connect the pump to a generator safely?

The pump is usually in the basement and the generator belongs outside — at least [20 feet from the house with the exhaust pointed away](/articles/how-far-should-generator-be-from-house). That distance dictates the connection:

- **Use one heavy-gauge outdoor extension cord** (12-gauge for runs up to about 50 feet, 10-gauge beyond that), plugged directly into the generator. Thin household cords waste voltage as heat, and a starved motor runs hot.
- **Never backfeed through the pump's wall outlet** or any outlet. If you want the pump on your home's wiring during outages, that's a transfer switch or interlock job — the [transfer switch versus interlock guide](/articles/transfer-switch-vs-interlock-kit) explains both.
- **Keep the connection dry.** The plug junction shouldn't sit in a puddle or on a wet basement floor. Elevate it on a bucket or a hook.
- **Test before the storm.** Start the generator, plug in the pump, and let it cycle twice. Discovering a surge problem in July beats discovering it in a hurricane.

## Should the sump pump be on your transfer switch?

If you're wiring any circuits for generator power, the sump pump should be first on the list — ahead of the TV, ahead of the microwave, ahead of almost everything except a medical device. Water damage compounds by the hour, and a flooded basement costs more than every other outage inconvenience combined.

Two practical points if you go this route. First, a switched circuit means the pump runs on generator power automatically when you flip over to backup — no extension cord snaked down the basement stairs, no plug to find in the dark. Second, tell your electrician the pump's *starting* watts, not just its running watts, when the circuits are being balanced. A panel loaded to its generator's limit with running loads will still trip when the pump's surge stacks on top of a furnace blower starting at the same moment. Leave surge headroom on the pump's circuit companions.

And if you add only one thing to the switched setup, make it a high-water alarm on the pit — a cheap sensor that screams when the water rises past the point where the main pump should have handled it. During an outage, that alarm is how you learn the generator's out of fuel while there's still time to fix it.

## The bottom line

Size for the surge, not the label. A 1/3 HP pump (1,300W surge) is happy on a 2,000-watt inverter; a 1/2 HP (2,150W surge) wants 3,500 watts in the real world; a 3/4 HP (3,000W surge) starts the conversation at 5,000. Then layer your protection the way water deserves: main pump, battery backup for the automatic hours, generator for the long siege. The homeowners who stay dry aren't the ones with the biggest generator — they're the ones whose setup doesn't need them to be home, awake, and lucky at the same time.
