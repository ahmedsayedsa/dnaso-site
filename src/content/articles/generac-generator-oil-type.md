**Quick answer:** A modern Generac home standby generator runs **conventional 5W-30 for its first 25 break-in hours**, then **Generac's full synthetic 5W-20 Gaseous Engine Oil (GEO)** at every change after that — that's Generac's current official spec, and it applies to both air-cooled and liquid-cooled home standby models. If the GEO oil isn't on the shelf, Generac accepts **full synthetic 5W-30** as the year-round alternative. The SAE 30 / 10W-30 temperature chart you'll find in older owner's manuals is legacy guidance — still printed in manuals in circulation, no longer the headline recommendation. Oil must meet **API service class SJ, SL, or better**.

If you've researched this question before, you've probably found three contradictory answers — and all three were correct at some point. This guide lays out the current spec, the legacy chart, and which one governs your machine, because Generac changed its recommendation and the internet hasn't caught up. For *when* to change it, see the companion guide: [how often to change Generac generator oil](/articles/how-often-change-generac-generator-oil).

## What oil does Generac officially recommend now?

Generac's current support documentation ("What type of oil should I use in my home backup generator?") is explicit, and it surprises owners who memorized the old chart:

| Oil | Temperature range | Generac's status |
|---|---|---|
| Conventional 5W-30 | Full range | Factory fill — **break-in only** (first 25 hours) |
| **Generac full synthetic 5W-20 GEO** | Full range | **Recommended after break-in, at every change** |
| Full synthetic 5W-30 | Full range | Acceptable alternative |
| 10W-30 | −10°F to 40°F (−23°C to 4°C) | Acceptable (emergency) |
| SAE 30 | Above 32°F (0°C) | Acceptable (emergency) |

Three details from that spec deserve emphasis:

**The generator ships with conventional 5W-30 on purpose.** Generac says not to switch to synthetic until after the initial 25-hour oil change — the conventional fill is the break-in oil, and the first change (covered in our [Generac oil change interval guide](/articles/how-often-change-generac-generator-oil)) is when the 5W-20 GEO goes in.

**The alternatives are emergencies, not equivalents.** Generac's framing is that synthetic 5W-30, 10W-30, and SAE 30 are for when the GEO oil is unavailable. In practice, full synthetic 5W-30 is what most owners and many dealers actually run — it meets the API requirement (SJ/SL or better), covers the full temperature range, and sits on every auto-parts shelf in America. The 10W-30 and SAE 30 entries are narrow-temperature fallbacks; I wouldn't choose them deliberately when a synthetic is available.

**"Gaseous Engine Oil" is a real distinction, not branding.** Your standby generator doesn't burn gasoline — it burns natural gas or propane vapor. Gaseous fuels run the oil differently (hotter valve-train environment, different deposit chemistry, no fuel dilution but more nitration), and the GEO formulation is Generac's answer for engines that spend their lives on gas. It's the same logic behind the older liquid-cooled manuals spec'ing their own fill, which brings us to the cooling question below.

## What did the older Generac manuals say?

The classic chart — printed in Guardian owner's manuals for years, and the source of most forum answers — reads like this:

| Ambient temperature | Legacy Generac recommendation |
|---|---|
| Above 40°F (4°C) | SAE 30 |
| 10°F to 40°F (−12°C to 4°C) | 10W-30 |
| Below 10°F (−12°C) | Synthetic 5W-30 |

Those manuals specified a high-quality detergent oil meeting **API service class SG, SH, SJ, or higher**, and the chart worked fine — millions of Generacs were serviced against it. Two things changed: Generac developed the GEO oil and unified the spec across the line, and full synthetics became cheap and universal. If your manual is an older printing, following its chart with a quality synthetic 5W-30 in cold country is not wrong — you're inside the legacy spec *and* the current acceptable list. What I'd retire is straight SAE 30 in any climate with real winters: it has no business being in a machine that must cold-start unattended in January.

## Is the oil different for air-cooled vs. liquid-cooled Generacs?

Short answer: **not anymore — with one historical footnote.**

Generac's current oil guidance explicitly covers both air-cooled and liquid-cooled home standby generators with the same recommendation: 5W-20 GEO after break-in. The footnote is that older liquid-cooled machines (the QuietSource and Protector lines of earlier generations) shipped from the factory with **15W-40** in the sump, and their manuals print a 15W-40-for-everything chart — a diesel-engine style oil for automotive-derived engines that run at 1,800 or 3,600 RPM liquid-cooled. If you own one of those units, your manual and the current support page genuinely disagree, and the manual that shipped with *your* engine is the tiebreaker until a dealer tells you otherwise. Don't switch a high-hour liquid-cooled engine to a lighter weight on the strength of a webpage — mine or Generac's.

## How much oil does a Generac take?

Residential air-cooled capacities span roughly **1.1 to 2.5 quarts** depending on model, with the popular Guardian sizes (10–24kW) clustering around **1.7–2.2 quarts with a filter change**. Generac publishes the exact figure per model in its support library and in your owner's manual specification table — use that, and always confirm on the dipstick rather than by the bottle count. Both directions of error matter on these engines: underfilled, you risk a low-oil-pressure shutdown mid-outage; overfilled, you can foul plugs and push oil past seals.

Practical tip from the field: buy the oil in a 5-quart jug rather than single quarts. Two changes' worth plus top-up oil, at a lower per-quart price — and a standby generator that burns a little oil during a 100-hour outage week needs that top-up bottle in the garage, not at a closed store.

And a habit worth building at every change: after refilling, run the generator for five minutes, shut it down, wait a few more, and check the dipstick again. A fresh filter absorbs oil as it primes, so the level you set on a dry sump drops slightly on the first run. Topping to the full mark after that first short run — not before — is what guarantees the level the engine actually lives with until the next service.

## Does the oil choice actually change how the generator behaves?

Yes — in two situations you'll feel rather than measure. The first is **cold starting**. A standby generator's hardest moment is an unattended crank at 5°F during a winter outage. Oil that's too thick for the temperature cranks slowly, builds pressure late, and can make the controller report a low-oil-pressure fault on an engine that has plenty of oil — the pressure switch reads pressure, not level, and cold SAE 30 delivers neither quickly. This is the practical reason Generac retired the warm-weather single-grades as the headline spec: a 5W synthetic flows at cranking speed in any weather your house will ever see.

The second is **long, hot runs**. Under sustained load in a summer outage, a conventional oil oxidizes and thins faster than a full synthetic; the synthetic's margin is exactly the multi-day storm scenario the machine exists for. Neither effect shows up in a weekly 5-minute exercise cycle, which is why owners who only ever see exercise runs conclude oil choice doesn't matter — right up until the first real outage grades the homework.

## What oil should you actually buy?

Decision logic, in order:

1. **Still in the first 25 hours?** Leave the factory conventional 5W-30 in until the break-in change. Don't early-switch to synthetic — Generac is explicit about this.
2. **After break-in, want the book answer?** Generac full synthetic **5W-20 GEO**, sold through dealers and online in Generac packaging.
3. **Can't get GEO, or maintaining it yourself on a budget?** Any name-brand **full synthetic 5W-30** meeting API SJ/SL or newer. This is the pragmatic default for most owners.
4. **Never:** conventional oil beyond break-in as a permanent fill, mystery-brand oil of any weight, or anything below API SJ. The savings are a few dollars a year against a five-figure machine (see what these cost installed in our [standby generator cost guide](/articles/standby-generator-cost) and [Generac generator cost guide](/articles/generac-generator-cost)).

One more storage note: oil doesn't expire on your shelf the way gasoline does, but an opened jug in a damp garage for three years isn't what I'd pour into a standby engine. Date the jug when you open it, and fold oil into the annual maintenance rhythm in our [generator maintenance checklist](/articles/generator-maintenance-checklist).

## The bottom line

Generac's oil story is simpler than the forums make it: conventional 5W-30 comes out at 25 hours, and full synthetic goes in — Generac's 5W-20 GEO by the book, or 5W-30 synthetic by the widely accepted alternative — and stays the answer in every temperature your house will ever see. The legacy temperature charts aren't wrong, just retired. Pick one synthetic, write it on the inside of the enclosure door with a paint pen, and the only oil question left is the interval — which is exactly what the companion guide answers: [how often to change Generac generator oil](/articles/how-often-change-generac-generator-oil).
