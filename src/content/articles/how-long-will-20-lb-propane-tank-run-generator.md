The 20-pound propane tank is the most common fuel canister in America — it's the grill tank, the one in every garage and under every patio. So when a dual-fuel generator owner asks about outage runtime, this is the tank they're picturing. Fair question, concrete answer: it depends almost entirely on load, and the honest range for a typical 5,000-watt dual-fuel generator is **about 9 hours loafing along at quarter load, down to under 4 hours working at full tilt.**

This guide shows the math, the full table, and the two gotchas that change the numbers in real life — exchange tanks that aren't actually full, and the load discipline that stretches any tank further.

## What's actually inside a 20-pound tank?

Less than the name suggests, and that's by design:

- **Capacity: 4.6 gallons of propane** when properly filled. Tanks are filled to 80% of water capacity so the liquid has room to expand on a hot day. The "20 lb" is the weight of the propane itself (propane weighs ~4.2 lb per gallon), not the weight of the tank — the empty steel tank adds another 16–18 pounds.
- **Energy: roughly 430,000 BTU total**, at about 91,500 BTU per gallon of propane.
- **Shelf life: effectively unlimited.** Unlike gasoline, propane doesn't go stale, absorb water, or gum anything. A tank filled in 2021 runs a generator in 2026 exactly as well. This is propane's superpower for backup power, and it's why dual-fuel owners forgive its lower energy per gallon.

For the gasoline and natural-gas side of the comparison, the [generator fuel consumption guide](/articles/generator-fuel-consumption/) has consumption by size and load across all three fuels, and the [dual-fuel versus gasoline versus propane comparison](/articles/dual-fuel-vs-gas-vs-propane-generator/) weighs the fuels head-to-head.

## How is generator propane runtime calculated?

Runtime is just division, with one number you have to get right:

**Runtime (hours) = usable gallons ÷ consumption (gallons per hour at your load)**

The catch is that consumption isn't fixed — it follows the load, because the engine burns fuel in proportion to the power it's producing. A dual-fuel generator around 5,000 watts (the most common size class for this question) burns propane at roughly:

- **~0.5 gal/hr at 25% load** (lights, fridge, TV, chargers)
- **~0.8 gal/hr at 50% load** (the realistic whole-house cruising load)
- **~1.2 gal/hr or more at full load** (everything on, AC cycling, well pump)

Those figures are typical for the class, not a promise for your exact model — your manual's spec sheet wins — but they're close enough to plan an outage around.

## How long will a 20-lb tank run a 5,000-watt generator?

Divide 4.6 gallons by each consumption rate:

| Load on a ~5,000W dual-fuel generator | Propane consumption | Runtime on one 20-lb tank (4.6 gal) |
|---|---|---|
| 25% load (~1,250W) | ~0.5 gal/hr | **~9 hours** |
| 50% load (~2,500W) | ~0.8 gal/hr | **~5.5–6 hours** |
| 100% load (~5,000W) | ~1.2+ gal/hr | **~3.5–4 hours** |

Read that table like an outage plan, because that's what it is. One grill tank gets a 5,000-watt generator through a night of essentials, or through half a day of heavy use. It does not get you through a two-day outage — that's a three-to-six-tank problem, which is why serious propane setups step up in tank size (next section).

Smaller generators sip less. A 2,000-watt dual-fuel inverter at half load burns roughly 0.25–0.3 gal/hr on propane, stretching one tank past 15 hours — the trade for that thrift is the limited load it can carry. Bigger machines drink proportionally more.

One more wrinkle: cold weather slightly reduces how fast a small tank can vaporize propane. In genuinely cold conditions (well below freezing) with a high-demand generator, a little 20-lb tank can struggle to feed the engine at full load — the tank frosts up and pressure sags. Another argument for the bigger tanks below if your outages come with winter attached.

## When should you step up to a 40-lb or 100-lb tank?

When your outage plan outgrows a single night. All three sizes connect the same way; you're only changing how often you swap:

| Tank | Propane capacity | Full weight | Runtime at 50% load on ~5,000W | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 20 lb (grill tank) | 4.6 gal | ~37 lb | ~5.5–6 hours | One night, light loads, maximum portability |
| 40 lb | 9.2 gal | ~72 lb | ~11–12 hours | A full day of essentials; still one-person movable (barely) |
| 100 lb | 23.6 gal | ~175 lb | ~29 hours | Multi-day outages; set it by the generator pad and leave it |

The 100-lb tank is the quiet hero of propane backup power: nearly a day and a half of half-load runtime per tank, better cold-weather vaporization, and refillable by the same propane service that fills home tanks in many areas. Two of them, rotated, cover most realistic outages without a single gasoline run. If your generator lives on a pad and runs through a [transfer switch or interlock](/articles/transfer-switch-vs-interlock-kit/), a big tank beside it is the closest a portable setup gets to standby convenience. Speaking of which — homes with a plumbed natural gas or bulk propane line skip this entire question; the [natural gas versus propane standby guide](/articles/natural-gas-vs-propane-standby/) covers that permanent-install world.

## Why do real runtimes beat the table at light loads?

Two reasons the math sometimes pleasantly surprises:

1. **Eco/throttle modes.** Inverter generators drop engine speed at light load and sip dramatically less than their rated consumption. A fridge-and-lights load at "25%" on paper may cruise well under it in practice.
2. **Loads cycle.** Your fridge compressor rests, the furnace blower stops between calls, the well pump sleeps. Real average load across an hour is usually lower than the sum you planned for — one more reason the [complete home backup power guide](/articles/complete-home-backup-power-guide/) preaches measuring your actual loads instead of guessing from nameplates.

The reverse surprise exists too: a cold tank at high load, an aging engine, or a swap-cage tank that started at 15 lb instead of 20 will all shave your hours. Plan with the table, then keep a spare tank as your rounding error.

## How does the math change on smaller and bigger generators?

The table above is built on the popular 5,000-watt class. Same tank, different machine:

| Generator (dual-fuel) | Typical propane burn at 50% load | Runtime on one 20-lb tank |
|---|---|---|
| 2,000W inverter | ~0.25–0.3 gal/hr | **~15–18 hours** |
| 3,500W portable | ~0.6 gal/hr | **~7.5 hours** |
| 5,000W portable | ~0.8 gal/hr | **~5.5–6 hours** |
| 7,500–9,000W portable | ~1.2–1.5 gal/hr | **~3–3.5 hours** |

The pattern worth remembering: runtime per tank is really about the *load*, and a big generator idling down at 1,500 watts burns close to what a mid-size one burns at the same output. Where the big machine costs you is when you actually use its capacity — and in its worse efficiency at very light loads, where it's dragging a bigger engine to do a small job. If your true outage load is a fridge, lights, and a [sump pump](/articles/generator-for-sump-pump/), a mid-size machine on propane is the endurance play.

## Can you mix propane and gasoline across a long outage?

Yes, and plenty of dual-fuel owners do exactly this — it's a legitimate strategy, not a compromise:

- **Propane first for the quiet hours.** Run propane overnight and during the light-load stretches: it's the cleaner, engine-friendlier fuel, and the tank doesn't care how long it sits between storms.
- **Gasoline for the heavy pushes.** When the big loads stack up (well pump + AC + laundry), gasoline's extra energy per gallon and the engine's full rated output earn their keep.
- **The real deadline is gasoline's, not propane's.** Burn your stored gasoline while it's fresh and keep the propane — which keeps forever — as the reserve that never expires. If the outage outlasts both plans, you're refilling the fuel that goes stale anyway.

One hard rule when switching: let the engine stop and cool a moment between fuels per your manual's procedure, and never open a fuel system on a hot engine. The manual's switching sequence exists because gasoline vapor and hot exhausts are a combination with no forgiveness in it.

## How do you stretch one tank as far as possible?

- **Run the generator in eco mode** if it has one, and let small loads accumulate before starting it.
- **Batch the big loads.** Run the well pump, microwave, and laundry in one generator session rather than idling the machine all day for the fridge alone.
- **Give the fridge hours off.** A closed fridge holds safe temperatures for about 4 hours, a full freezer for 24–48. Cycling the generator — two hours on, two off — can nearly double your tank's calendar life during a long outage.
- **Keep the tank honest.** Weigh it before storm season; top it up at a refill station, not a swap cage; store it upright, outdoors, never in a basement or garage (a leaking tank indoors pools an invisible, explosive gas at floor level).
- **Mind the placement rules** while you're at it: generator at least [20 feet from the house](/articles/how-far-should-generator-be-from-house/), tank connected where it sits — never bring the generator closer to shorten the hose.

## The bottom line

One 20-lb tank = 4.6 gallons = roughly **9 hours at quarter load, 6 at half, under 4 flat-out** on a typical 5,000-watt dual-fuel generator. That's a night of essentials — a genuinely useful unit of backup power, and exactly one unit. Size your tank farm to your outage history: a spare 20 for short storm country, a 40 or 100 for the multi-day stuff, and refills instead of exchanges so the gallons on the label are the gallons you actually get.
